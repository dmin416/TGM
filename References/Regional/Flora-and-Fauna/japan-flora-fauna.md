# Japan — Flora and Fauna

---

## Overview

Japan's archipelago spans from subtropical Okinawa in the south to subarctic Hokkaido in the north, producing enormous ecological diversity across a relatively small total land area. The mountain spine running the length of Honshu creates sharp climatic divisions between the Pacific coast (mild, rainy) and the Sea of Japan coast (heavy winter snow, cold). Over seventy percent of Japan's territory is forested mountain, leaving the coastal plains and river valleys intensely cultivated. Marine resources are enormous due to the convergence of the cold Oyashio and warm Kuroshio currents off Japan's Pacific coast.

## Flora

### Trees and Forest

**Japanese Cedar (Sugi)** *(Cryptomeria japonica)* — The most widely planted timber tree in Japan. Ancient groves line the approaches to major shrines (Nikko, Kasuga Taisha). Planted in plantation forestry across the mountains since the Edo period. In 1913 sugi is the primary construction timber for traditional buildings. In modern times massive plantation forestry has created a severe national pollen allergy problem affecting millions each spring.

**Japanese Cypress (Hinoki)** *(Chamaecyparis obtusa)* — The prestige timber of Japan. Used for shrine and temple construction, quality furniture, wooden baths and high-end interiors. Its distinctive fragrance is culturally associated with purity and quality. Ancient hinoki forests are largely depleted by 1913; plantation-grown hinoki fills the gap. The scent of hinoki is immediately recognizable in traditional Japanese architecture in both 1913 and modern times.

**Cherry (Sakura)** *(Prunus serrulata and many species)* — The national flower of Japan. The Yoshino cherry (Somei Yoshino) dominates landscape plantings across Japan, all clonally propagated. Cherry blossom viewing (hanami) is the most important secular seasonal celebration in Japan. In 1913 hanami is already a major cultural institution with beer, bento and crowds under the trees. In modern times the sakura front is tracked daily by national weather services and the blooming of the cherry is a national event.

**Japanese Maple (Momiji)** *(Acer japonicum, A. palmatum)* — Mountain and garden tree celebrated for brilliant autumn foliage. Momiji viewing (koyo) is the autumn equivalent of hanami. Maple leaf imagery appears in family crests, textiles and lacquerware. Present in 1913 and modern times as both wild forest tree and cultivated garden specimen.

**Bamboo** *(Phyllostachys and Pleioblastus species)* — Ubiquitous across central and southern Japan. Shoots (takenoko) eaten in spring. Culms used for construction scaffolding, garden fencing, water pipes, baskets, fans, lacquerware backing and countless other uses. Bamboo groves are culturally associated with purification and spirituality (Arashiyama bamboo grove in Kyoto). In modern times bamboo is still harvested and the Arashiyama grove is a major tourist destination.

**Japanese Black Pine (Kuromatsu)** *(Pinus thunbergii)* — Coastal pine on sandy shores and rocky headlands throughout Japan. Wind-shaped forms are iconic in Japanese landscape art. Used extensively in classical garden design. Associated with longevity and is a primary species for bonsai cultivation. Present in 1913 and modern times.

**Paulownia (Kiri)** *(Paulownia tomentosa)* — Fast-growing lowland tree. The imperial chrysanthemum crest of the emperor and the paulownia crest of the government (used by the Prime Minister's office and on Japanese passports) make this the most heraldically significant plant in Japan. Timber extremely lightweight and used for tansu chests and wooden sandal (geta) bases. In 1913 paulownia chests are planted by families when a daughter is born, to be harvested for her wedding chest when mature. In modern times the practice continues in some rural areas.

**Ginkgo (Icho)** *(Ginkgo biloba)* — Temple and street tree throughout Japan. Brilliant yellow autumn color. The ginkgo is the symbol of Tokyo. Nuts (ginnan) sold roasted at autumn festivals. Ancient specimens at major shrines considered sacred. In modern times every major Japanese city plants ginkgo as a street tree.

### Shrubs and Garden Plants

**Camellia (Tsubaki)** *(Camellia japonica)* — Native to Japan's temperate forests. Large-flowered winter-to-spring bloomer. Seed oil (tsubaki oil) used for hair care, blade preservation and cooking. The camellia is associated with samurai culture (the flower drops whole rather than petal by petal, analogous to decapitation). In 1913 camellia oil is a standard hair and metal care product. In modern times tsubaki oil is a premium beauty product sold internationally.

**Wisteria (Fuji)** *(Wisteria floribunda)* — Climbing vine with spectacular hanging purple flower clusters in spring. Fuji is the reading that gives Fujiwara (wisteria field) its name. Wisteria trellises (fuji棚) at major shrines and parks draw crowds in 1913 and modern times.

**Chrysanthemum (Kiku)** *(Chrysanthemum morifolium)* — The imperial flower of Japan. The sixteen-petaled chrysanthemum is the imperial crest. Chrysanthemum viewing (kiku) is an autumn festival. Petals eaten in vinegared salads (kiku no sunomono). In 1913 chrysanthemum cultivation for exhibition is an elaborate art form. In modern times the imperial crest appears on passports and official documents.

**Japanese Plum (Ume)** *(Prunus mume)* — The plum blossom blooms before the cherry in early spring, often in snow. Associated with perseverance and strength. Fruit processed into umeboshi (salt-pickled plum) and umeshu (plum liqueur). Plum blossom viewing (ume matsuri) is a secondary spring festival at major shrines. In 1913 and modern times umeboshi is a daily condiment in Japanese cuisine.

**Lotus (Hasu)** *(Nelumbo nucifera)* — Temple pond plant throughout Japan. Buddhist symbolism (purity rising from mud). Seeds, roots (renkon) and leaves all used in cuisine. Present in 1913 and modern times; renkon is a standard Japanese vegetable.

### Medicinal and Useful Plants

**Wasabi** *(Wasabia japonica)* — Native to cold mountain stream banks in Japan. Rhizome grated fresh for a volatile, sharp heat that dissipates quickly. An anti-parasitic condiment for raw fish. Genuine wasabi is expensive and perishable; in 1913 only high-quality establishments use fresh wasabi. In modern times most wasabi served outside Japan (and much within it) is horseradish paste dyed green.

**Sansho Pepper** *(Zanthoxylum piperitum)* — Japanese mountain shrub. Pods produce a citrusy numbing sensation similar to Sichuan peppercorn but less intense. Ground dried pods used on grilled eel (unaju) and in seven-spice blend (shichimi togarashi). Young leaves (kinome) used as aromatic garnish. Present in 1913 and modern times.

**Shiso / Perilla** *(Perilla frutescens)* — Green and red varieties grown in every Japanese garden. Green leaves eaten with sashimi, in tempura and as sushi wrapping. Red leaves used to color umeboshi. Aromatic and slightly medicinal. Unchanged in modern times as a daily culinary herb.

**Mugwort (Yomogi)** *(Artemisia princeps)* — Wild on hillsides and roadsides throughout Japan. Young spring leaves pounded into mochi for yomogi mochi (green rice cake). Used in traditional moxibustion (okyu). Identical uses in 1913 and modern times.

**Cryptomeria bark and cedar extracts** — Used in traditional medicine for their antimicrobial properties. Applied to wounds. The same resins used in traditional building also have medicinal applications known to rural practitioners.

## Fauna

### Large Mammals

**Japanese Macaque (Nihonzaru)** *(Macaca fuscata)* — The northernmost non-human primate in the world. Distributed across Honshu, Shikoku and Kyushu. Famous in modern times for the hot-spring-bathing population at Jigokudani in Nagano. In 1913 macaques are common in mountain forests and occasional crop raiders. Culturally prominent in the three wise monkeys (mizaru, kikazaru, iwazaru) iconography at Nikko Toshogu.

**Japanese Serow (Kamoshika)** *(Capricornis crispus)* — Endemic Japanese ungulate. Stocky goat-antelope in mountain forests. National Special Natural Monument in modern times. Present in mountain forest zones in 1913.

**Asian Black Bear (Tsukino wa Guma)** *(Ursus thibetanus japonicus)* — Japanese subspecies of the Asian black bear. Present on Honshu, Shikoku and formerly Kyushu. White crescent on chest. Occasional human attacks in forested areas. Present in 1913; population reduced but surviving in modern times. Hokkaido has the brown bear (Ezo Higuma) instead.

**Hokkaido Brown Bear (Ezo Higuma)** *(Ursus arctos yesoensis)* — Large brown bear subspecies on Hokkaido. Apex predator. Occasionally attacks humans and livestock. In 1913 subject to intensive hunting by Hokkaido settlers. Still present in Hokkaido in modern times; population managed.

**Japanese Deer (Shika)** *(Cervus nippon)* — The native deer of Japan. Sacred at Nara, where hundreds of free-roaming deer interact with visitors at Kasuga Taisha shrine. Population expanded dramatically in modern times due to hunting restrictions and predator absence. In 1913 managed but common in forested and mountain areas.

**Japanese Wild Boar (Inoshishi)** *(Sus scrofa leucomystax)* — Present throughout Japan except Hokkaido. Farmed and hunted for meat (botan nabe, wild boar hot pot). Crop raider. Present in 1913 and increasingly problematic in modern times as rural depopulation expands forest margins.

### Birds

**Japanese Crane (Tancho)** *(Grus japonensis)* — Symbol of longevity, fidelity and good fortune in Japanese culture. Appears in art, family crests and the folded paper crane (origami) tradition. Breeding population in Hokkaido; winters in Izumi, Kagoshima. In 1913 the Hokkaido population is severely depleted by hunting and wetland loss. In modern times protected; Hokkaido population recovered to several thousand individuals.

**Japanese Pheasant (Kiji)** *(Phasianus versicolor)* — The national bird of Japan. Male brilliant green-blue with red facial wattles. Found in fields, woodland edges and mountain foothills throughout Japan except Hokkaido. Hunted for meat and sport. Appears in Momotaro folklore. Present in 1913 and modern times.

**Japanese Bush Warbler (Uguisu)** *(Horornis diphone)* — The "nightingale of Japan." Heard but rarely seen; sings from dense undergrowth in spring. Its distinctive song (hoo-hokekyo) is the sound of early spring in Japan. Used as a metaphor for spring's arrival in poetry since the Man'yoshu. In 1913 and modern times the uguisu song is one of the most culturally loaded sounds in Japanese aesthetic tradition.

**Japanese White-Eye (Mejiro)** *(Zosterops japonicus)* — Small bird with distinctive white eye ring. Feeds on flower nectar and small insects. Its seasonal movements track flower blooming. Frequently depicted in woodblock prints alongside plum and cherry blossoms. Present in 1913 and modern times.

**Mandarin Duck (Oshidori)** *(Aix galericulata)* — Symbol of marital devotion in Japanese culture. Breeds in forested mountain streams. Present in 1913 and modern times.

### Marine and Freshwater

**Pacific Bluefin Tuna (Kuro Maguro)** *(Thunnus orientalis)* — The apex fish of Japanese cuisine. Fatty belly (otoro) is the most prized cut in all of Japanese food culture. Caught in coastal and offshore waters. In 1913 tuna is consumed fresh only near landing ports; refrigerated rail transport is developing. In modern times tuna is consumed across Japan and globally; wild stocks severely depleted.

**Pacific Salmon (Sake)** *(Oncorhynchus species)* — Major protein source in Hokkaido and northeastern Honshu. Salted for preservation (shiozake), grilled, made into ikura (salmon roe) and consumed fresh. In 1913 salted salmon is a standard preservation food for inland markets. In modern times salmon is the most consumed fish in Japan by volume.

**Japanese Eel (Unagi)** *(Anguilla japonica)* — Freshwater eel grilled in kabayaki style (split, skewered, grilled and basted with sweet soy-mirin sauce). Considered a summer stamina food especially on Doyo no Ushi no Hi (midsummer day of the ox). Unagi restaurants are a distinct restaurant category in 1913 and modern times. Wild unagi population critically endangered in modern times; aquaculture dominant but dependent on wild-caught juveniles.

**Oyster (Kaki)** *(Crassostrea gigas)* — Cultivated in sheltered bays along the Seto Inland Sea and northeastern coast. Eaten raw, grilled, fried (kaki furai) and in nabe. In 1913 oyster cultivation is already well-developed in Hiroshima Bay. In modern times Hiroshima is the largest oyster producing region in Japan.

**Abalone (Awabi)** *(Haliotis gigantea)* — Harvested by ama divers (female free divers) along the coasts of Mie, Shizuoka and other prefectures. Sliced thin and eaten raw or braised. A prestige food in 1913 and modern times. The ama tradition is a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage.

**Fugu (Pufferfish)** *(Takifugu rubripes and relatives)* — Poisonous pufferfish eaten for their delicate white flesh. The tetrodotoxin in the liver and ovaries is lethal; preparation requires a government license in modern times (licensing began 1958). In 1913 fugu is eaten by skilled preparers; accidental poisoning deaths occur. The cultural thrill of eating something potentially lethal is part of the appeal in both periods.

## Notes

Japan's geographical isolation as an archipelago produced a uniquely endemic flora and fauna with many species found nowhere else. The islands' diverse climate zones compressed into short latitudinal distances created extraordinary biodiversity. The cultural integration of seasonal flora into aesthetic and religious life is probably deeper in Japan than in any other culture: the cherry and maple calendars, the lotus pond, the pine and bamboo and plum combination (sho-chiku-bai, the three friends of winter), the crane and turtle as longevity symbols are all omnipresent in visual culture, poetry, architecture and ceremony in 1913 and modern times.
