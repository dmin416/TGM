# Korea — Flora and Fauna

---

## Flora

### Trees and Forests

**Korean Pine** *(Pinus koraiensis)* — Dominant across northern and alpine zones. Seeds edible and traded. Timber used for construction and furniture. Still present in modern times in protected mountain forest reserves.

**Ginkgo** *(Ginkgo biloba)* — Planted widely across temple grounds and village centers for centuries. Considered sacred. Nuts harvested in autumn, roasted and eaten or used medicinally. Identical presence in modern Korea; street-planted in every major city.

**Korean Maple** *(Acer pseudosieboldianum)* — Deciduous hardwood across mountain slopes. Brilliant autumn color. Timber used for tool handles and small furniture. Still present in modern times across mountain parks.

**Zelkova** *(Zelkova serrata)* — Large shade tree planted in village squares and near shrines. Considered spirit-inhabited in folk belief. Extremely old specimens survive into modern times as protected natural monuments.

**Wild Cherry** *(Prunus serrulata)* — Hillside and mountain forest tree. Blooms heavily in spring. Bark used in traditional medicine. The same species celebrated in modern Korea as spring flowering tree.

**Korean Fir** *(Abies koreana)* — High-altitude conifer on Hallasan and northern peaks. Resin used medicinally. Endangered in modern times due to climate change on mountain peaks.

**Bamboo** *(Phyllostachys and Pleioblastus species)* — Dense groves in southern provinces, especially South Jeolla. Shoots eaten in spring. Culms used for baskets, furniture, arrows and household tools. Present in modern times; industrially cultivated in south.

**Paulownia** *(Paulownia tomentosa)* — Fast-growing lowland tree. Timber prized for furniture and musical instruments, especially gayageum and haegeum soundboards. Planted near homes. Still cultivated in modern times for timber.

**Persimmon** *(Diospyros lotus / kaki)* — Orchard and dooryard tree throughout the peninsula. Fruit dried into gotgam in winter. Tannin from unripe fruit used to waterproof paper and fabric. Identical role in modern Korea.

**Mulberry** *(Morus alba)* — Cultivated throughout lowlands to feed silkworms. Leaves harvested repeatedly through summer. Fruit eaten fresh or fermented. Sericulture industry present in 1913; reduced but not gone in modern times.

### Shrubs and Understory

**Azalea** *(Rhododendron species, especially R. mucronulatum)* — National flower of Korea. Hillsides carpeted in pink and purple each spring. Flowers eaten in hwajeon pancakes during spring festivals. Toxic to livestock in quantity.

**Korean Forsythia** *(Forsythia koreana)* — Endemic shrub flowering bright yellow in early spring before leaves emerge. Planted as ornamental in 1913 gardens and in modern times across every Korean city.

**Wild Rose** *(Rosa rugosa, R. multiflora)* — Coastal and hillside scrub. Hips collected in autumn for tea and medicine. Flowers used in confections.

**Hawthorn** *(Crataegus pinnatifida)* — Fruit used heavily in traditional medicine and cooking. Dried and made into tea or candied. Planted near farmhouses. Present in modern times in orchards and traditional medicine markets.

**Korean Spice Viburnum** *(Viburnum carlesii)* — Fragrant spring-flowering shrub in mountain understory. Ornamental in temple gardens.

### Medicinal and Cultivated Plants

**Korean Ginseng** *(Panax ginseng)* — The most economically significant medicinal plant in Korea. Cultivated under shade frames in Gaeseong and Geumsan regions. Roots dried and traded across Asia and into Europe. Considered the premier tonic plant in East Asian medicine. In 1913 a major export commodity. In modern times a multi-billion dollar industry; Geumsan remains the ginseng capital.

**Mugwort** *(Artemisia princeps)* — Used in moxibustion, rice cakes (ssuk tteok) and soup. Grows wild on every hillside and roadside. Considered a protective herb. Identical uses in modern Korea.

**Bellflower Root** *(Platycodon grandiflorus)* — Root eaten as banchan after salting and squeezing. Used medicinally for respiratory complaints. Flowers ornamental blue-purple. Still a standard side dish in modern Korean cuisine.

**Perilla** *(Perilla frutescens)* — Cultivated in every kitchen garden. Leaves eaten fresh, pickled or used to wrap meat. Seeds pressed for oil used in cooking and lamp fuel in 1913. Identical use in modern times.

**Angelica** *(Angelica gigas)* — Deep purple-stemmed medicinal herb. Root used in traditional medicine for pain and women's complaints. Young shoots eaten as namul. Present in mountain markets in modern times.

**Acanthopanax** *(Eleutherococcus senticosus)* — Adaptogenic root used in traditional medicine similar to ginseng but cheaper and more common. Leaves eaten as namul in spring. Modern supplement industry uses this plant heavily.

**Lotus** *(Nelumbo nucifera)* — Cultivated in temple ponds and wetlands. Seeds, roots and leaves all eaten. Leaves used to wrap food for steaming. Temple cuisine features this plant heavily in 1913 and in modern times.

**Chrysanthemum** *(Chrysanthemum morifolium)* — Cultivated for tea, medicine and edible flowers. Used in autumn festival foods. Petals floated in ceremonial wine. Present in modern times in tea form.

### Coastal and Wetland

**Sea Mustard / Miyeok** *(Undaria pinnatifida)* — Kelp harvested from coastal waters. Dried and eaten in soup. Miyeokguk consumed by new mothers after childbirth and on birthdays. Dominant seaweed in Korean diet in 1913 and modern times.

**Gim / Laver** *(Pyropia yezoensis)* — Harvested from intertidal rocks or cultivated on coastal frames. Dried into sheets and roasted with sesame oil and salt. Eaten with every meal in modern Korea. Already a major coastal industry in 1913.

**Glasswort / Haemul Namul** *(Salicornia europaea)* — Salty marsh plant eaten raw or pickled along tidal flats.

## Fauna

### Large Mammals

**Amur Tiger** *(Panthera tigris altaica)* — Present in northern Korea and Manchurian border zones in 1913. Japanese colonial hunting campaigns and habitat loss eliminated tigers from the peninsula by the 1920s. Extinct in wild Korea in modern times. Survives in Russian Far East and Chinese borderlands.

**Amur Leopard** *(Panthera pardus orientalis)* — Mountain forest predator in northern provinces in 1913. Effectively extinct on the Korean peninsula in modern times. Critically endangered globally.

**Asian Black Bear** *(Ursus thibetanus)* — Mountain forests throughout the peninsula in 1913. Population severely reduced by hunting and deforestation. Small wild population survives in Jirisan in modern times under protection.

**Korean Water Deer** *(Hydropotes inermis argyropus)* — Distinctive fanged deer native to Korea and China. Found in riverside reed beds and agricultural margins. No antlers. Males have elongated canine teeth. Still present in modern South Korea; population stable.

**Siberian Roe Deer** *(Capreolus pygargus)* — Mountain and forest deer throughout the peninsula. Hunted for meat and hide in 1913. Still present in forested areas of modern Korea.

**Wild Boar** *(Sus scrofa)* — Ubiquitous throughout mountain and forest zones. Hunted for meat. Crop raider. Present in modern Korea in increasing numbers as forests regrow.

---

### Birds

**Red-crowned Crane** *(Grus japonensis)* — Symbol of longevity and good fortune in Korean culture. Wintered in the Han River estuary and coastal wetlands. Severely reduced by 1913 due to wetland loss. Small wintering population at Cheorwon and DMZ wetlands in modern times.

**Eurasian Magpie** *(Pica pica sericea)* — National bird of Korea. Present in every village and city in 1913 and modern times. Considered a bringer of good news.

**Oriental Stork** *(Ciconia boyciana)* — Large wetland bird nesting in riverside trees in 1913. Extinct as breeding bird in South Korea by 1970s. Reintroduction program ongoing in modern times at Yesan.

**White-naped Crane** *(Antigone vipio)* — Wintered in Korean wetlands. Still winters in DMZ area in modern times.

**Mandarin Duck** *(Aix galericulata)* — Symbol of marital fidelity in Korean culture. Breeds in mountain forest streams. Present in modern Korea.

---

### Marine

**Pacific Cod** *(Gadus macrocephalus)* — Major commercial fish in 1913. Dried into bugeo and hwangtae, two of the most important preserved fish in Korean cuisine. Still commercially fished in modern times though stocks reduced.

**Yellow Croaker** *(Larimichthys polyactis)* — The most culturally important fish in Korean cuisine. Salted, dried or eaten fresh. Overfished severely in modern times; wild stocks drastically reduced from 1913 levels.

**Abalone** *(Haliotis discus hannai)* — Harvested by female divers (haenyeo) in Jeju and southern coastal waters. Considered a luxury food in 1913. Aquaculture dominant in modern times; wild harvest expensive.

**Pacific Oyster** *(Magallana gigas)* — Cultivated in southern coastal bays. Eaten raw, grilled or in soup. Major aquaculture product in modern times.

---

## Notes

The Korean peninsula in 1913 retains substantial old-growth forest in the northern mountain zones and Manchurian border regions. Japanese colonial forestry begins aggressive logging in this period. By modern times the north retains more intact forest through lower industrialization while the south has reforested extensively since the 1960s from a heavily denuded postwar baseline. The DMZ functions as an unintended wildlife refuge in modern times, hosting populations of species extirpated elsewhere on the peninsula.
