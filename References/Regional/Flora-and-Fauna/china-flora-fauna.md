# China — Flora and Fauna

---

## Overview

China's territory in 1913 spans an extraordinary range of climates and ecosystems: tropical rainforest in Yunnan and Hainan, temperate broadleaf forest across the central and eastern regions, boreal forest and steppe in Manchuria and Inner Mongolia, high-altitude plateau and alpine desert on the Tibetan Plateau, and subtropical coast from Shanghai south to Guangdong. The flora and fauna vary accordingly. This file covers major species by ecological zone with notes on their cultural and practical significance.

---

## Flora by Region

### North China Plain and Yellow River Basin

**Millet** *(Setaria italica, Panicum miliaceum)* — The original staple grain of northern China. Cultivated for over eight thousand years. Still grown in Shanxi, Shaanxi, Gansu and Hebei in 1913 as a primary food crop. Porridge (zhou), steamed cakes and fermented spirit base. In modern times largely replaced by wheat and rice as a daily staple but still cultivated and prized.

**Winter Wheat** *(Triticum aestivum)* — The dominant grain crop of the North China Plain in 1913. Grown through winter and harvested in early summer. Flour milled for noodles, steamed buns (mantou), dumplings (jiaozi). The dietary foundation of the north. Unchanged in modern times.

**Kaoliang / Sorghum** *(Sorghum bicolor)* — Tall drought-tolerant grain grown across the northern plains and Manchuria. Eaten as porridge and steamed grain. Fermented into baijiu (notably Maotai style). Important fuel and construction material (stalks). Still cultivated in modern times primarily for liquor production.

**Chinese Elm** *(Ulmus parvifolia)* — Street and farmland windbreak tree across the northern plain. Extremely hardy. Bark ground to flour during famines as starvation food. Present in 1913 and modern times.

**Jujube** *(Ziziphus jujuba)* — Red date tree. Cultivated in Hebei, Shanxi and Shandong. Fruit eaten fresh in autumn, dried for year-round use in medicine and cooking. Sweetness without refined sugar. One of the oldest cultivated fruits in China. Unchanged in modern times.

**Chinese Wolfberry / Goji** *(Lycium barbarum)* — Shrub with small red berries grown in Ningxia and Gansu. Used in medicinal soups and teas as a tonic for vision and vitality. In 1913 a regional specialty traded through traditional medicine networks. In modern times exported globally as a superfood.

### Yangtze River Basin and Central China

**Lotus** *(Nelumbo nucifera)* — Cultivated in ponds and shallow lakes throughout the Yangtze basin. Root (lotus root, ou) eaten as vegetable. Seeds eaten fresh or dried. Leaves used to wrap and steam food. Every part used. Unchanged in modern times.

**Tea** *(Camellia sinensis)* — The most economically significant plant in Chinese history. Grown in Fujian, Zhejiang, Hunan, Sichuan, Yunnan and Anhui. Green tea (longjing, biluochun), white tea (Fujian), black tea (keemun, Yunnan dian hong), oolong (Wuyi, Anxi), pu-erh (aged fermented, Yunnan). In 1913 China's largest agricultural export. In modern times still a massive industry; China is the world's largest tea producer.

**Bamboo** *(Phyllostachys edulis and many species)* — Dominant understory plant across southern and central China. Shoots eaten in spring. Culms used for construction, furniture, paper, cooking utensils, weapons. Giant panda food source. In 1913 bamboo scaffolding is universal in Chinese construction. In modern times bamboo industry includes engineered lumber and textiles.

**Ginkgo** *(Ginkgo biloba)* — Temple and street tree throughout China. Native to China; only wild populations known from Zhejiang. Nuts eaten roasted and in congee. Medicinally used for circulation and memory. In 1913 ancient specimens stand at famous temples. In modern times the species is planted globally; China's ancient trees are national monuments.

**Chinese Chestnut** *(Castanea mollissima)* — Mountain forest tree and orchard crop. Nuts roasted by street vendors in autumn across every Chinese city in 1913. Unchanged in modern times as a street food.

**Peach** *(Prunus persica)* — Native to China. Cultivated since antiquity. Symbolizes longevity and immortality in Chinese culture. The peach is the fruit of the immortals (xiantao) in mythology. Grown across the central and northern regions. Unchanged in modern times.

**Osmanthus** *(Osmanthus fragrans)* — Flowering shrub grown for intensely fragrant small flowers. Used to flavor tea, wine, cakes and confections. The osmanthus blossom is the city flower of Hangzhou, Guilin and other cities. Blooms in autumn. In 1913 osmanthus wine and osmanthus cake are festival foods. In modern times osmanthus flavoring is used commercially across Chinese food and tea industries.

### South China and Guangdong

**Lychee** *(Litchi chinensis)* — Subtropical fruit tree native to Guangdong and Fujian. Pink-red rough-skinned fruit with white translucent flesh. Famous since Tang dynasty imperial tribute. In 1913 available fresh in southern markets in early summer; dried or candied for trade inland. In modern times widely grown and exported.

**Longan** *(Dimocarpus longan)* — Subtropical fruit closely related to lychee. Smaller, brown-skinned. Called "dragon eye." Dried longans used in medicinal soups and desserts. Grown in Guangdong, Fujian and Guangxi. Unchanged in modern times.

**Sugarcane** *(Saccharum officinarum)* — Grown in Guangdong, Guangxi and Yunnan. Pressed for juice drunk fresh from roadside stalls. Processed into raw sugar for trade. In 1913 sugarcane juice stalls are common in southern cities in winter. In modern times China is a major sugar producer.

**Star Anise** *(Illicium verum)* — Native to Guangxi and Vietnam border region. Dried seed pods used in five-spice powder and braised meat dishes throughout China. Source of shikimic acid used in Tamiflu production in modern times. In 1913 traded throughout China and exported.

**Chinese Banyan** *(Ficus microcarpa)* — Dominant shade tree in southern Chinese cities and village squares. Aerial roots descend to form secondary trunks. Sacred in folk belief. Ancient specimens considered spirit-inhabited. In 1913 every Guangdong village has a banyan-shaded public space. In modern times the banyan is the city tree of Guangzhou and Fuzhou.

### Yunnan and Southwest

**Yunnan Pine** *(Pinus yunnanensis)* — Dominant highland conifer across Yunnan plateau. Resin collected and traded. Timber widely used. Still present in modern Yunnan forests though reduced by logging.

**Camellia** *(Camellia japonica and relatives)* — Wild camellias native to Yunnan forests. Camellia oil pressed from seeds used for cooking and hair care. Ornamental cultivation in gardens. Yunnan is the center of wild camellia diversity. In modern times Yunnan camellias are major ornamental plants globally.

**Rhododendron** *(Rhododendron species)* — Enormous diversity across Yunnan highlands. Shrubs and trees in alpine zones. Many species with large flowers in white, pink, red and purple. In 1913 European plant hunters are actively collecting Yunnan rhododendrons for introduction to European gardens. In modern times Yunnan is the global center of rhododendron diversity.

**Opium Poppy** *(Papaver somniferum)* — Grown across Yunnan, Sichuan, Guizhou and parts of the northwest in 1913. Despite Qing dynasty prohibition efforts the crop remains widespread. Following the Opium Wars the trade is partly legalized and then attempts at suppression follow. In 1913 cultivation continues particularly in remote highland areas. In modern times opium production in Chinese territory is eliminated; the Golden Triangle (Burma, Laos, Thailand border) is the regional production zone.

### Manchuria and Northeast

**Manchurian Walnut** *(Juglans mandshurica)* — Dominant forest tree in Manchurian river valleys. Nuts edible but strongly flavored. Timber used for furniture. Still present in modern northeast China forests.

**Korean Pine** *(Pinus koraiensis)* — Mixed forest with deciduous broadleafs across Manchuria and the Korean border region. Seeds (pine nuts) eaten and traded. Major timber tree. Reduced but present in modern northeast forests.

**Amur Cork Tree** *(Phellodendron amurense)* — Bark used in traditional Chinese medicine (huangbai) for infection and inflammation. Forest tree in Manchuria and northeast. Still harvested in modern times for traditional medicine.

**Wild Soybean** *(Glycine soja)* — The wild ancestor of the cultivated soybean, native to Manchuria and northeastern China. Manchuria is the world's largest soybean producer in 1913 and a major factor in great power competition for the region. Soybeans crushed for oil and pressed into bean cake sold as fertilizer across Asia. In modern times China is a massive soybean importer despite domestic production.

## Fauna

### Large Mammals

**Giant Panda** *(Ailuropoda melanoleuca)* — Mountain bamboo forests of Sichuan, Shaanxi and Gansu. Extremely rare and little studied in 1913; largely unknown outside China except through specimens. Hunted occasionally. In modern times the global symbol of wildlife conservation; wild population around 1800 individuals under strict protection.

**South China Tiger** *(Panthera tigris amoyensis)* — Subtropical forests of Fujian, Guangdong, Hunan and Jiangxi in 1913. Still hunted as a threat to livestock and humans. Population severely reduced through the 20th century. Functionally extinct in the wild in modern times; a small captive breeding population exists.

**Amur Tiger** *(Panthera tigris altaica)* — Manchurian forests in 1913. Present in significant numbers in the border forests between Manchuria and Russia. By modern times the Manchurian population is nearly eliminated; survives primarily in Russian territory with a small cross-border population in Jilin.

**Snow Leopard** *(Panthera uncia)* — Tibetan Plateau and western mountain ranges. Hunted for fur in 1913. In modern times protected; China holds the largest national population of snow leopards globally.

**Giant Panda's ecological range correlates directly with cloud forest and bamboo zones.** Any scene set in Sichuan or adjacent highland areas in this period could plausibly include rare sightings referenced by local guides.

**Red Panda** *(Ailurus fulgens)* — Cloud forest in Yunnan, Sichuan and Himalayan foothills. Small, arboreal, bamboo-feeding. Better known than the giant panda in European natural history circles in 1913 as it was described first. In modern times endangered; wild population under 10,000.

**Père David's Deer** *(Elaphurus davidianus)* — Originally native to the North China Plain marshes and river deltas. Extinct in the wild by 1900 due to hunting and habitat loss. Surviving population kept in the imperial hunting park at Nanyuanlu near Beijing. In modern times reintroduced from European zoo stock; small wild and semi-wild populations exist in Jiangsu and Hubei.

**Chinese Alligator** *(Alligator sinensis)* — Yangtze River lowlands, particularly Anhui and Jiangsu. Critically rare even in 1913 due to hunting and wetland loss. In modern times functionally extinct in the wild; captive breeding programs operate in Anhui.

**Baiji / Yangtze River Dolphin** *(Lipotes vexillifer)* — Endemic freshwater dolphin in the Yangtze River. Present in 1913; known and revered in riverside communities as the "goddess of the Yangtze." In modern times declared functionally extinct in 2006 following rapid industrialization of the river.

**Yangtze Finless Porpoise** *(Neophocaena asiaeorientalis)* — Freshwater porpoise of the Yangtze. More robust population than the baiji in 1913. In modern times critically endangered with population under 1000 individuals.

### Birds

**Red-crowned Crane** *(Grus japonensis)* — Manchurian marshes and Yangtze delta wetlands. Symbol of longevity and immortality in Chinese art and poetry. Appears constantly in paintings and decorative arts across all periods. Still present in modern times; population around 2800 individuals globally.

**Chinese Pheasant** *(Chrysolophus pictus, Golden Pheasant)* — Sichuan and Shaanxi mountain forests. Brilliant gold and red plumage. A frequent motif in Chinese decorative art. Introduced to European aviaries and game estates in the 19th century. Still present in wild Sichuan populations in modern times.

**Mandarin Duck** *(Aix galericulata)* — Forest streams and lakes across central and eastern China. Male plumage is extraordinary in complexity. Symbol of marital fidelity and happiness in Chinese culture. Present in paintings, embroidery and decorative arts universally. In modern times present in suitable habitat; also introduced to Britain and western Europe where feral populations breed.

---

### Marine and Freshwater

**Chinese Sturgeon** *(Acipenser sinensis)* — Yangtze River and East China Sea. Enormous fish reaching several hundred kilograms. Caviar and flesh highly valued. In 1913 still present in the Yangtze in significant numbers. In modern times critically endangered; spawning migration blocked by dams.

**Grass Carp, Silver Carp, Bighead Carp, Common Carp** — The four domesticated carp of Chinese aquaculture, polyculture-raised in ponds across China since antiquity. The foundation of Chinese freshwater fish production in 1913 and modern times. Introduced globally; considered invasive in North America.

---

## Notes

China's biodiversity in 1913 is substantially greater than in modern times due to less industrialization, lower population density in sensitive ecosystems and less systematic habitat conversion. The Sichuan basin, Yunnan highlands and Manchurian forests still contain large contiguous habitat. The Yangtze River system has not yet been dammed (Three Gorges Dam completed 2006). The North China Plain is heavily farmed but still has wetland margins along the Yellow River. The subsequent century of industrialization, land conversion, overhunting and dam construction causes the most severe biodiversity loss in Chinese history.
