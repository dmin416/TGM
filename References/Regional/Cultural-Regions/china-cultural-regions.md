# China — Cultural Regions

---

## Political Context 1913

The Qing dynasty collapsed in 1911. The Republic of China was proclaimed on January 1, 1912. Yuan Shikai became President. In TGM's divergent timeline communist ideology exists but no successful communist state has been established; China's spiritual and religious traditions remain intact rather than being suppressed. The warlord fragmentation that follows Yuan Shikai's death in 1916 is the likely near-term trajectory. China in 1913 is a nation in acute political crisis: the imperial administrative system has collapsed, the new republican institutions are fragile, regional military governors (governors-general) hold de facto power, and foreign powers maintain concessions, treaty ports and extraterritorial privileges across the country.

The mage pool estimate (8,600 mages from 430 million people) is described as a sleeping giant no European power has figured out how to access. Chinese spiritual traditions are the mechanism through which Chinese mages develop ability. This is significant for the story.

## Major Cultural Regions

### North China (华北)

**Core provinces:** Zhili (Hebei), Shandong, Shanxi, Shaanxi, Henan

**Capital:** Beijing (called Peking by Western powers; Beiping briefly after 1912 when the Republic moved its capital to Nanjing, but Beijing remains the actual political center under Yuan Shikai)

**Character:** The heartland of Chinese imperial civilization. The Yellow River basin is where Chinese agriculture and written culture originated. Confucianism is strongest here. The northern plain is densely farmed wheat and millet land. The loess plateau of Shanxi and Shaanxi is heavily eroded, poor and prone to famine. Coal and iron ore deposits in Shanxi make it strategically important.

**Beijing specifically:** The Forbidden City, the Altar of Heaven, the Summer Palace and the Temple of Confucius still stand. The Legation Quarter houses foreign diplomatic missions behind walls. The old Manchu bannermen neighborhoods are in decline. The city is beginning to modernize with electric streetcars and modern shops alongside ancient hutong alley neighborhoods. Foreign influence is concentrated here. Yuan Shikai's government operates from Beijing.

**Social character:** The traditional scholar-gentry class is strongest in the north. Confucian examination culture shaped everything until 1905 when the exams were abolished. The men who built careers on classical learning are now without a system. New schools teaching Western subjects are opening but the transition is raw and contentious.

**In modern times:** Beijing is the capital of the People's Republic. The Forbidden City is a museum. The hutong neighborhoods have been largely demolished and replaced. Industrial and service economy. Population over 20 million. Shandong is a major industrial and agricultural province. Shanxi remains a coal mining center.

### Northeast China / Manchuria (东北)

**Provinces:** Fengtian (Liaoning), Jilin, Heilongjiang

**Major cities:** Mukden (Shenyang), Harbin, Dalian (leased to Russia, then Japan)

**Character:** The Manchu homeland. Ethnically and culturally distinct from Han China for centuries. In 1913 undergoing rapid agricultural settlement by Han Chinese migrants from Shandong and Hebei (chuang Guandong: "rushing through the pass"). The Russian-built Chinese Eastern Railway and South Manchuria Railway create an industrial corridor. Harbin in 1913 is a cosmopolitan city with a large Russian, Jewish and European commercial community due to Russian railway construction. The Japanese have a strong military and commercial presence in the south following the 1905 Russo-Japanese War. Manchuria's soybean production and mineral resources make it one of the most contested territories in East Asia.

**Mage significance:** The Manchurian border forests are spiritually significant in indigenous Tungusic shamanic traditions. Manchu shamanism (saman) is a distinct and still-practiced tradition in 1913 among older Manchu families, though cultural Sinicization has been extensive.

**In modern times:** The three northeastern provinces are an industrial rust belt following the collapse of Soviet-era heavy industry. Harbin retains a distinct architectural character from the Russian period. The region has lost population as younger workers migrate south.

### Central China / Yangtze Basin (华中)

**Provinces:** Hubei, Hunan, Jiangxi, Anhui

**Major cities:** Wuhan (the tri-city of Hankou, Wuchang, Hanyang), Changsha, Nanchang

**Character:** The middle Yangtze region. Wuhan is the commercial crossroads of China where the north-south Han River meets the east-west Yangtze. The 1911 revolution that overthrew the Qing began with the Wuchang Uprising here. The region is politically active and turbulent. Lake Dongting, Lake Poyang and the Yangtze flood plain are among the most agriculturally productive areas in China. Rice double-cropping, cotton, sesame and rapeseed cultivation.

**Mage significance:** The Wudang Mountains in Hubei are a major Taoist sacred mountain complex. Taoist cultivation traditions practiced at Wudang are among the most sophisticated in China. Internal alchemy (neidan), breath cultivation (qigong precursors), and martial cultivation traditions based on internal energy (qi) development originate here. If Chinese mages exist in TGM, Wudang-style cultivation is a primary mechanism.

**In modern times:** Wuhan is a major industrial city of over 11 million people. The Three Gorges Dam (completed 2006) sits upstream in western Hubei. Changsha is a rapidly growing city with strong tech and media industries.

### Lower Yangtze / Jiangnan (江南)

**Provinces:** Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Shanghai (treaty port city)

**Major cities:** Shanghai, Nanjing, Hangzhou, Suzhou, Ningbo

**Character:** The wealthiest and most sophisticated region in China. "Jiangnan" (south of the river) means refinement, luxury and classical culture in Chinese cultural imagination. Silk production, refined cuisine, garden architecture, classical poetry and painting. Suzhou and Hangzhou are the archetypes of Chinese aesthetic civilization. Shanghai in 1913 is already an extraordinary hybrid: the International Settlement and French Concession are self-governing foreign territories within the city limits, with their own police, laws and administration. The Bund along the Huangpu River is lined with European bank buildings. Chinese and foreign commercial culture coexist and collide.

**Shanghai in 1913:** The largest city in Asia by some measures. The most important commercial port in China. A center of comprador culture (Chinese merchants who bridge Western and Chinese commercial worlds). New journalism, publishing, theater and political activism. The city's cosmopolitan character makes it the most European-accessible Chinese city and the entry point for Western ideas, goods and technology into China.

**Mage significance:** Jiangnan is home to major Buddhist temple complexes and Taoist shrines. The region's prosperity funded elaborate religious establishments. The West Lake at Hangzhou and the monastery on nearby Lingyin Temple are significant spiritual sites.

**In modern times:** Shanghai is China's financial center and largest city. Suzhou and Hangzhou are wealthy modern cities preserving classical garden districts. The Jiangnan region is the economic heart of China.

### South China / Lingnan (岭南)

**Provinces:** Guangdong, Guangxi, Fujian, Hainan

**Major cities:** Guangzhou (Canton), Hong Kong (British colony), Fuzhou, Xiamen, Nanning

**Character:** The region south of the Nanling mountains. Linguistically distinct: Cantonese, Hokkien (Fujian), Hakka and other languages are mutually unintelligible with Mandarin and with each other. The primary source of the Chinese diaspora: the Cantonese and Hokkien communities in Southeast Asia, the Americas and Australia in 1913 originated overwhelmingly from Guangdong and Fujian. Hong Kong under British rule is a separate administrative entity. The Guangdong coast is dense with treaty ports and foreign commercial presence.

**Mage significance:** Lingnan has distinct spiritual traditions including Taoist folk practice and popular religion fusing Buddhist, Taoist and indigenous deity worship. The region's open contact with the outside world has produced syncretic religious cultures not found in the more conservative north.

**Revolutionary significance:** Sun Yat-sen is from Guangdong. The 1911 revolution had strong Guangdong participation. The overseas Chinese commercial networks that funded republican revolutionary movements are largely Guangdong and Fujian origin. The region is politically the most active in Chinese republican politics in 1913.

**In modern times:** Guangdong is China's wealthiest province by GDP. The Pearl River Delta (Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Dongguan, Zhuhai) is the world's largest manufacturing cluster. Hong Kong (returned to China 1997) operates under "one country, two systems." Cantonese culture remains distinct.

### Southwest (西南)

**Provinces:** Sichuan, Yunnan, Guizhou, and adjoining areas

**Major cities:** Chengdu, Chongqing, Kunming, Guiyang

**Character:** Mountainous, geographically isolated, ethnically diverse. Sichuan is a fertile basin surrounded by mountains: the Chengdu Plain is one of the most productive agricultural areas in China. Yunnan contains enormous ethnic diversity (Yi, Bai, Naxi, Dai, and dozens of other groups) and borders Burma, Laos and Vietnam. Guizhou is the poorest province in China in 1913, mountainous and inaccessible.

**Mage significance:** The southwest is the most spiritually diverse region in China. Yi shamanic traditions, Bai Benzhu religion, Naxi Dongba shamanism (with its own pictographic script still in use in 1913 for ritual texts), Dai Theravada Buddhism and Tibetan Buddhism in the western reaches. The region's geographic isolation has preserved traditions suppressed or attenuated elsewhere. If Chinese spiritual traditions are the mechanism for mage development in TGM, Yunnan and the southwest are the richest source.

**The Naxi Dongba tradition** — The Naxi people of Lijiang practice a shamanic religion (Dongba) with a functioning pictographic writing system (only pictographic script still in active use in the world in 1913). Dongba priests are ritualists who memorize long texts and perform ceremonies for birth, death, healing and harvest. This tradition is a plausible analog for a sophisticated pre-modern mage tradition in TGM's framework.

**In modern times:** Chongqing is a megacity of over 30 million and a direct-controlled municipality. Chengdu is a major tech and cultural center. Lijiang is a major tourism destination with a preserved Naxi old town. Yunnan's biodiversity attracts scientific and ecotourism interest globally.

### Northwest (西北)

**Provinces and territories:** Shaanxi, Gansu, Xinjiang, Ningxia, Qinghai, and the Tibetan Plateau

**Major cities:** Xi'an, Lanzhou, Urumqi

**Character:** The Silk Road corridor. Xi'an (ancient Chang'an) was the Tang dynasty capital and the eastern terminus of the Silk Road. The northwest is predominantly Muslim (Hui ethnicity) in the river valleys and oasis towns, and Turkic Muslim (Uyghur and other groups) in Xinjiang. Tibetan Buddhist culture dominates the plateau. The region is China's most ethnically and religiously diverse and least governed from Beijing in 1913. Large sections of Xinjiang and Tibet are under nominal Qing/Republic control but administered by local authorities.

**Mage significance:** Islamic spiritual traditions (Sufi orders, including the Naqshbandiyya active in Gansu and Xinjiang), Tibetan Buddhist tantra and Vajrayana practice (elaborate visualization, mantra and deity yoga traditions), and Mongolian shamanism all coexist in this region. The density of highly developed spiritual cultivation traditions in the northwest is potentially the highest in China.

**In modern times:** The northwest is the site of China's most politically sensitive ethnic and religious tensions. Xinjiang's Uyghur population faces severe repression. Tibet's political status remains contested. Gansu and Qinghai contain large Tibetan and Hui populations. The Silk Road tourism corridor attracts international visitors.

## Social Structure 1913

### The Educated Gentry Class (Shēnshì)

The traditional ruling class defined by classical education and examination success. With the abolition of the imperial examinations in 1905 this class lost its institutional basis but not its social prestige or land wealth. In 1913 the surviving gentry is fragmenting: some have enrolled in new Western-style schools and entered modern professions, some have become local military strongmen, some cling to classical education as cultural identity.

### Compradors

Chinese merchants who mediate between Western commercial interests and the Chinese market. Highly educated in both Chinese and Western commercial practices. Often bilingual. Concentrated in treaty port cities. Wealthy, culturally hybrid, politically influential. The most visible Chinese face of modernization and foreign commerce in 1913.

### Military Men (Junfa)

The rising class. Control of armies means control of territory means revenue. The collapse of the Qing left military governors as the effective rulers of provinces. Yuan Shikai is the ultimate expression of this. Warlordism accelerates after 1916.

### Peasant Farmers

The vast majority of the population. Subsistence and small-market agriculture. Subject to landlord rent, tax collection, military conscription and periodic famine. The condition of the peasantry is the central social problem of Republican China. Life expectancy is low. Literacy is minimal.

## Religion and Spiritual Traditions

**Popular Religion** — The dominant religious practice of ordinary Chinese people is a syncretism of Buddhism, Taoism and folk deity worship without rigid denominational boundaries. Temple festivals, deity veneration, fortune telling, geomancy (fengshui) and ancestor veneration are universal practices. The spirit world is densely populated with local earth gods (tudi gong), city gods (chenghuang), kitchen gods (zaoshen) and countless other deities.

**Buddhism** — Present in Chinese culture for nearly two millennia. Chan (Zen) Buddhism, Pure Land Buddhism and Esoteric Buddhism all have practitioners. Monasteries on famous mountains (Wutai, Emei, Putuo, Jiuhua) are major religious centers. Lay Buddhist associations are growing in 1913 as part of a reform movement.

**Taoism** — Indigenous Chinese philosophical and religious tradition. The major orders (Quanzhen in the north, Zhengyi in the south) maintain temple networks. Taoist cultivation practices (internal alchemy, meditation, breath work, ritual performance) are sophisticated systems developed over centuries. In TGM's framework these practices are the most plausible mechanism for Chinese mage development.

**Confucianism** — More an ethical and social system than a religion. Provides the framework for family relations, social hierarchy, political theory and education. Its institutional basis (the examination system) has collapsed but its cultural influence remains comprehensive.

**Islam** — Practiced by Hui (Chinese-speaking Muslims) throughout China and by Turkic peoples in Xinjiang. Significant Sufi order activity in Gansu and Ningxia. The Hui community is integrated into Chinese society while maintaining distinct religious practice.

**Christianity** — Protestant missionaries (American, British, German) have been active since the mid-19th century. Catholic missions older still. By 1913 Christianity has a presence in every province. Concentrated in coastal cities, Shandong (American Presbyterian missions) and Guangdong. Associated with modern hospitals, schools and universities.

## Language

**Mandarin (Guanhua / Putonghua)** — The lingua franca of educated Chinese across the country. Based on the Beijing dialect. Used in government, education and inter-regional communication. In 1913 not yet standardized or universally taught; regional educated elites use it alongside local languages.

**Regional Languages** — Cantonese, Shanghainese (Wu), Hokkien, Hakka, Min Nan and dozens of others are mutually unintelligible languages (not dialects in the technical sense). A Cantonese merchant and a Shanghainese merchant cannot communicate in their native languages. Educated Chinese communicate in written classical Chinese or spoken Mandarin.

**Classical Chinese (Wenyan)** — The written language of the educated class. Highly compressed, grammatically different from spoken Chinese, standardized across centuries. Official documents, literature and formal correspondence are in classical Chinese in 1913. The vernacular writing movement (baihua) is gaining momentum but not yet dominant.

**In modern times:** Mandarin (Putonghua) is universal as a second language throughout China through decades of mandatory education. Regional languages survive in home and community use but young urban Chinese often have weak proficiency in their ancestral regional language.
