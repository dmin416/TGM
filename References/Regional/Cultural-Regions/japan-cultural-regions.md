# Japan — Cultural Regions

---

## Political Context 1913

Japan in 1913 is the Taisho era (Emperor Yoshihito ascended 1912). The Meiji transformation (1868-1912) has produced a modern industrial state with a constitution, elected parliament (Diet), conscript army and navy modeled on German and British lines, and a rapid industrialization program. Japan has defeated China (1895) and Russia (1905) in major wars. Korea is annexed (1910). Japanese Manchurian influence through the South Manchuria Railway and the Kwantung Leased Territory is substantial.

In TGM's divergent timeline Korea retains independence through German backing, creating a direct point of friction between Germany and Japan. Japan's strategic posture in 1913 is expansionist: the Korea situation is an irritant and Japanese military planners regard Korean independence as a temporary obstacle. Japan's alliance with Britain (Anglo-Japanese Alliance, 1902) is the central diplomatic fact of its international position.

## Major Regions

### Kanto (関東)

**Prefectures:** Tokyo, Kanagawa, Saitama, Chiba, Ibaraki, Tochigi, Gunma

**Capital and center:** Tokyo (formerly Edo)

**Character:** The political and economic heart of modern Japan. Tokyo became the capital when Emperor Meiji moved from Kyoto to Edo in 1869. In 1913 Tokyo is a city of roughly two million people, rapidly modernizing. Western-style government buildings, department stores (Mitsukoshi), electric streetcars, newspapers and universities coexist with traditional merchant townhouses (machiya), temples, and the dense working-class shitamachi (downtown) neighborhoods of the old Edo city.

The Imperial Palace sits at the center on the former grounds of Edo Castle. Ginza is already the premier shopping and entertainment district. Asakusa is the popular entertainment district with its temple, theaters and street food. Ueno houses museums, the zoo and Shinobazu Pond. Yokohama (Kanagawa) is the primary international port with a large foreign settlement.

**Military significance:** The Army General Staff headquarters and the naval command structures are in Tokyo. The major military academies are in and around Tokyo. Strategic decisions about Korea and China are made here.

**In modern times:** Greater Tokyo is home to 35-38 million people, the largest metropolitan area on Earth. The city rebuilt after the 1923 Great Kanto Earthquake and again after 1945 firebombing. The Yamanote Line circle train defines the inner city geography. Financial, media, tech and government functions are concentrated here to a degree unmatched by any other major country.

### Kansai / Kinki (関西)

**Prefectures:** Osaka, Kyoto, Hyogo (Kobe), Nara, Shiga, Wakayama, Mie

**Centers:** Osaka (commercial), Kyoto (cultural), Kobe (international port)

**Character:** The historical heartland of Japanese civilization. Kyoto was the imperial capital for over a millennium (794-1869). Nara was the capital before that. The great Buddhist temple complexes (Todaiji, Horyuji, Kofukuji), Shinto shrines (Fushimi Inari, Kasuga Taisha), and the remains of ancient palace sites are concentrated in the Kinai (home provinces) region.

Kyoto in 1913 retains its role as Japan's cultural capital even though the emperor has moved. The old court aristocracy (kuge) families and their cultural traditions persist. Traditional arts (Noh theater, Kodo incense ceremony, tea ceremony, ikebana, Nishijin silk weaving, Kiyomizu pottery) are centered here. Geisha quarters (hanamachi) in Gion and Pontocho remain active and influential.

Osaka is the commercial city. The Osaka merchant culture (konamon, down-to-earth pragmatism, emphasis on value and food) is distinct from Tokyo's bureaucratic and Kyoto's aristocratic cultures. Osaka merchants funded much of the economic activity of the Edo period. In 1913 Osaka is industrializing rapidly with textile mills and heavy industry.

Kobe is the most international city in Japan after Yokohama. The foreign settlement (Kitano district) contains Western-style residences and churches. Chinese and Korean communities are present. In 1913 Kobe is the premier port for Western trade.

**In modern times:** The Keihanshin (Kyoto-Osaka-Kobe) metropolitan area has 20 million people. Kyoto is Japan's premier cultural tourism destination with the densest concentration of UNESCO World Heritage Sites in any single city. Osaka is a major industrial and commercial city known for its food culture and direct humor. Kobe maintains a cosmopolitan and international character.

### Chubu (中部)

**Prefectures:** Aichi (Nagoya), Shizuoka, Nagano, Niigata, Toyama, Ishikawa, Fukui, Gifu, Yamanashi

**Center:** Nagoya (Aichi)

**Character:** Japan's geographic and productive middle. Nagoya in 1913 is a major castle city becoming an industrial center. The Nobi Plain around Nagoya is intensively farmed. The Japan Alps (three ranges: Hida, Kiso and Akaishi) run through the center of Chubu, creating dramatic mountain terrain that divides the Pacific coast (mild, rainy) from the Sea of Japan coast (heavy winter snow).

Niigata on the Sea of Japan coast is the major port facing Korea and Siberia. Its rice production is the finest in Japan. The snow country (yukiguni) culture of the Sea of Japan coast has a distinct aesthetic of endurance and introspection; this is the landscape of Kawabata Yasunari's Snow Country.

Shizuoka produces most of Japan's green tea. The view of Mount Fuji from the Tokaido road between Tokyo and Kyoto has been the defining landscape of Japan in art and popular culture since the Edo period. In 1913 Fuji is visible on clear days from both Tokyo and the Tokaido.

**In modern times:** Nagoya is Japan's third-largest city and the center of Toyota's automotive empire. Toyota City (formerly Koromo) is a short distance from Nagoya. The region is Japan's most significant manufacturing cluster. Niigata is famous for rice and sake. The Japan Alps are major ski and hiking destinations.

### Tohoku (東北)

**Prefectures:** Aomori, Iwate, Miyagi (Sendai), Akita, Yamagata, Fukushima

**Character:** The northeast of Honshu. Historically Japan's frontier, settled and developed later than the west and center. The Ainu indigenous people maintained territory in northern Tohoku until forced southward in antiquity. Cold climate with heavy Sea of Japan snow on the western side. Rice paddies in river valleys are the agricultural foundation. Sendai (Miyagi) is the major city, a castle town of the Date clan.

Tohoku culture is characterized by perseverance, reserve and directness; qualities associated with the harsh climate. Major summer festivals (matsuri): the Nebuta float festival (Aomori), Tanabata decorations (Sendai), the Kanto pole-lantern festival (Akita) are among the most spectacular in Japan.

**In modern times:** Tohoku is the region most severely affected by the March 2011 earthquake and tsunami. The Pacific coast communities were devastated; recovery continues. The region's rural population is declining with outmigration to cities. Agricultural and fishing industries are the economic base.

---

### Chugoku and Shikoku (中国・四国)

**Major cities:** Hiroshima, Okayama, Matsuyama, Kochi

**Character:** The western end of Honshu and the small island of Shikoku across the Seto Inland Sea. The Inland Sea itself is a sheltered body of water with over three thousand islands, famous for mild weather and excellent seafood. The eighty-eight temple pilgrimage of Shikoku (Shikoku Hachijuhakkasho) following the circuit of temples associated with the Buddhist monk Kukai (Kobo Daishi) is one of Japan's most important pilgrimage traditions, conducted on foot in 1913 by pilgrims in white robes.

Hiroshima in 1913 is a major military base city (the site of the military district headquarters and a major Army embarkation port). Its later history does not apply in 1913.

**In modern times:** Hiroshima is defined by its atomic bombing history and the Peace Memorial. The Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum and Genbaku Dome are visited by millions. Shikoku is the least developed of Japan's four main islands with a significant rural depopulation problem.

---

### Kyushu (九州)

**Prefectures:** Fukuoka, Saga, Nagasaki, Kumamoto, Oita, Miyazaki, Kagoshima

**Character:** Japan's southernmost main island. The closest Japanese territory to Korea and China. Historically the entry point for continental influence: Buddhism arrived through Kyushu, as did Portuguese Jesuits in the 16th century (Nagasaki was the sole legal trading port for two centuries of Edo isolation). Korean and Chinese cultural influence is stronger here than anywhere else in Japan outside Okinawa.

Fukuoka (Hakata) in 1913 is the primary port for trade with Korea and China. The city has a large Korean merchant community. The Dazaifu government complex nearby was Japan's ancient administrative center for western affairs and Korea relations. In TGM's framework Fukuoka would be a significant observation point for German-Japanese tensions over Korea.

Nagasaki in 1913 still bears the character of its centuries as Japan's only trading port. The Dejima island site where Dutch traders were confined, the Chinatown (Shinchi), the hill neighborhoods of former foreign residents, and the large Christian community (descendants of Hidden Christians who preserved faith through the persecution period) give Nagasaki a cosmopolitan and layered history unlike anywhere else in Japan.

Kagoshima (formerly Satsuma domain) contributed the key military figures of the Meiji Restoration. The Shimazu clan's domain produced the men who overthrew the Tokugawa shogunate.

**In modern times:** Fukuoka is the fastest-growing major Japanese city and serves as a gateway to Asia. Its startup culture and compact urban design are nationally recognized. Nagasaki is a major destination for historical tourism and atomic bomb memory (bombed August 9, 1945). Kagoshima is the departure point for Yakushima (ancient cedar forest, UNESCO World Heritage Site).

---

### Okinawa and the Ryukyu Chain (沖縄)

**Character:** The Ryukyu Kingdom was an independent maritime trading state until formally annexed by Japan in 1879. The Ryukyuan language, culture and identity are distinct from mainland Japanese. Okinawan martial arts (Ryukyu kempo, which gave rise to karate), Ryukyuan music (sanshin, a three-stringed instrument related to the Chinese sanxian), Okinawan textile traditions (bingata dyeing, bashofu banana fiber cloth) and folk religion (noro priestess tradition) are all distinct from Japanese mainland culture.

In 1913 Okinawa is under Japanese administration but the cultural distinctiveness is recent and raw. The older generation speaks Ryukyuan languages. Japanese language education is standardizing the younger generation but bilingualism is still widespread.

**In modern times:** Okinawa is a special case in Japanese politics due to the disproportionate burden of US military bases (approximately 70 percent of US forces in Japan are based on Okinawa, which is 0.6 percent of Japan's land area). The base presence and Okinawan cultural distinctiveness fuel a persistent independence and autonomy movement. Okinawa is Japan's poorest prefecture and a major beach tourism destination.

## Social Structure 1913

### The Imperial House

Emperor Taisho (Yoshihito) ascended in 1912. The emperor is constitutionally a sovereign in the Prussian-influenced Meiji Constitution but actual governance is conducted by the genro (elder statesmen from the Meiji Restoration) and cabinet. In 1913 the genro system is weakening as the Meiji generation ages. The military is increasingly independent of civilian political control.

### Genro (Elder Statesmen)

The surviving architects of the Meiji state. By 1913 most of the original genro are dead or retiring. Yamagata Aritomo (Army faction) and Saionji Kinmochi (party government faction) are the primary surviving genro. Their influence is enormous but the system will not survive their generation.

### Kazoku (Nobility)

The Meiji-created peerage. Former daimyo (feudal lords), court aristocracy and Meiji-era meritocrats combined into a Western-style House of Peers. Five ranks: Prince, Marquis, Count, Viscount, Baron. A functioning aristocratic class with political roles in the upper house.

### Bureaucratic and Military Elite

The graduates of Tokyo Imperial University (Todai) and the military academies (Army War College, Naval War College) constitute the governing elite. Competitive examination into the higher civil service and officer training produces a meritocratic but intensely hierarchical educated class. In 1913 this class is taking over from the Meiji oligarchs.

### Merchant and Industrial Class

The major zaibatsu (financial-industrial conglomerates) are forming in this period: Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, Yasuda. These family-controlled conglomerates span banking, shipping, manufacturing and trading. The zaibatsu relationship with the military and government is symbiotic and mutually reinforcing.

### Commoners (Heimin)

The vast majority of the population. Farmers, fishermen, urban workers, craftsmen, small merchants. Universal male conscription means every young man does military service. Universal elementary education has produced high literacy rates by 1913 standards. Urban migration is accelerating as industry pulls rural labor into cities.

### Burakumin

Descendants of the former outcaste class (eta and hinin). Legally emancipated in 1871 but subject to severe social discrimination in 1913. Concentrated in specific occupations and neighborhoods. The discrimination persists into modern times in attenuated form.

## Religion 1913

**Shinto** — Japan's indigenous religion. Worship of kami (spirits or gods) present in natural phenomena, ancestors and significant places. The state promoted a version of Shinto (kokka shinto, State Shinto) as a national ideology connecting the emperor to divine lineage (Amaterasu, the sun goddess). Every community has its ujigami (local tutelary shrine). Major national shrines: Ise Jingu (Inner and Outer Shrines dedicated to Amaterasu, the most sacred site in Japan), Izumo Taisha (marriage deity), Meiji Jingu (Tokyo, completed 1920 for Emperor Meiji).

**Buddhism** — Present in Japan for 1400 years. Major schools: Jodo (Pure Land), Jodo Shin (True Pure Land), Zen (Rinzai and Soto), Nichiren, Shingon (esoteric). Temple registration system under Edo meant virtually all Japanese were formally Buddhist. In 1913 a complex relationship with the state: the Meiji government briefly promoted Shinto at Buddhism's expense (shinbutsu bunri, separation of kami and buddhas) but popular Buddhism remains dominant in funeral rites and ancestor veneration.

**Christianity** — A small but influential minority. Protestant missions (American Methodist, Presbyterian) and Catholic missions. Associated with modernization and Western education. A disproportionate number of early Meiji reformers and intellectuals were Christian or Christian-influenced.

**New Religions** — Meiji-era syncretic new religions: Tenrikyo, Konkokyo and others. Growing rapidly among the disrupted populations of rapid industrialization.

## Language

**Japanese** — Agglutinative language with no demonstrable genetic relatives (though structural similarities with Korean exist). An extensive honorific system (keigo) with distinct vocabulary levels for plain speech, polite speech and formal speech. The appropriate register signals social relationship immediately. Using the wrong level is a serious social error.

**Writing** — Japanese is written in a combination of three scripts used simultaneously. Kanji (Chinese characters adapted for Japanese use): several thousand in common use. Hiragana (syllabic script for Japanese grammatical elements): 46 characters. Katakana (syllabic script for foreign words and emphasis): 46 characters. In 1913 full literacy requires mastery of all three plus familiarity with classical Chinese texts for the educated class. Newspaper literacy is high by 1913 due to Meiji-era mass education.

**Regional Languages** — Ryukyuan languages (Okinawa), Ainu (Hokkaido, rapidly disappearing in 1913 as Ainu people are forced to assimilate), and regional dialects of Japanese (Tohoku, Kansai, Kyushu) that are mutually intelligible but significantly different in phonology, vocabulary and grammar.

## Key Cultural Practices

**Meiji-Era Modernization** — The cultural whiplash of the Meiji period is still fresh in 1913. Men in the government and military wear Western suits and uniforms. Traditional kimono and hakama coexist with Western dress in daily life. Western architecture dominates government buildings while traditional architecture remains in homes, temples and residential neighborhoods. Western music, theater and arts have been introduced alongside traditional forms. The tension between Western modernity and Japanese tradition (wakon yosai: Japanese spirit, Western technology) is the defining cultural negotiation of the Meiji and Taisho eras.

**The Aesthetic Tradition** — Japanese aesthetic concepts (wabi: quiet imperfection, sabi: the beauty of age and weathering, mono no aware: the pathos of things and impermanence, ma: negative space and interval, yugen: mysterious depth) permeate art, architecture, garden design, poetry (haiku, tanka), theater (Noh, Kabuki) and craft. These are not abstract philosophical concepts but lived aesthetic practices that shape how educated Japanese perceive and evaluate everything. In 1913 these concepts are fully operative. In modern times they underlie Japanese design internationally.

**Onsen (Hot Spring) Culture** — Japan's volcanic geology produces thousands of hot spring baths throughout the country. Communal bathing in onsen is a major social and leisure institution in 1913 and modern times. Every region has its distinctive spring type (sulfur, iron, sodium bicarbonate). Onsen ryokan (hot spring inns) are the definitive Japanese travel experience in both periods. Public bathing (sento) in cities for those without private baths is universal in 1913.

**Matsuri (Festivals)** — Every shrine has its annual festival. Major festivals involve processions (mikoshi, portable shrines carried through the streets), music, dance, food stalls and community participation. The summer bon festival (Obon) honors returning ancestral spirits with dance (bon odori). New Year (Shogatsu) is the most important holiday with shrine visits (hatsumode), special foods and family gatherings. These practices are identical in character from 1913 to modern times.
