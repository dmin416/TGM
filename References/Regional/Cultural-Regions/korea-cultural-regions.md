# Korea — Cultural Regions

---

## Political Context 1913

Korea (Joseon Dynasty, renamed Korean Empire in 1897) was annexed by Japan in 1910. In TGM's divergent timeline Korea retains independence backed by German military support due to its Aetherium deposit. The Joseon court structure and Korean imperial administration remain intact. Japanese commercial and diplomatic pressure continues but military annexation has not occurred. The Gwangmu Emperor (Gojong) or his successor remains on the throne. The traditional eight-province administrative structure (Paldo) remains the basis of regional identity.

---

## The Eight Provinces (Paldo)

### Gyeonggi Province — 경기도

**Center:** Seoul (Hanseong / Gyeongseong), the capital

**Character:** The political, administrative and cultural heart of Korea. The royal palace complexes (Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, Deoksugung) anchor the city. The yangban aristocracy is densest here. Confucian academies (seowon) and government examination culture define the educated class. Korean government bureaus, foreign legations and modernizing institutions all concentrate in Seoul. The Han River divides the city from the southern suburbs.

**Economy:** Government service, commerce, craft production, river trade. The surrounding Gyeonggi lowlands produce rice for the capital.

**Social character:** Conservative Confucian hierarchy at the top. A growing merchant and artisan class beneath. Foreign presence (Japanese, Chinese, a small number of European diplomats and missionaries) is concentrated here. German military advisors would be stationed in or near Seoul.

**In modern times:** Seoul is the dominant megalopolis of South Korea, home to roughly half the national population. The palace complexes are preserved as national monuments. The Han River is flanked by expressways and apartment blocks.

---

### Chungcheong Province — 충청도

**Divided into:** North Chungcheong (Chungbuk) and South Chungcheong (Chungnam)

**Character:** Inland agricultural region south of the capital. Known as the home of the most refined Confucian scholarship and conservative literati culture. The phrase "Chungcheong yangban" implies the most traditional and formal behavioral standards in Korea. Less commercially developed than Gyeonggi or the coastal provinces.

**Economy:** Rice farming, soybean cultivation, ginseng growing (particularly Geumsan), inland fishing.

**Notable:** Baekje dynasty historical heartland. Ancient royal tombs at Buyeo and Gongju. Buddhism and Confucianism coexist in the intellectual culture.

**In modern times:** Daejeon is the major city. Sejong City was built from scratch as a government administrative center. Geumsan remains Korea's ginseng center. The region is known for conservative and formal social norms that persist into modern times.

---

### Jeolla Province — 전라도

**Divided into:** North Jeolla (Jeonbuk) and South Jeolla (Jeonnam)

**Character:** The richest agricultural region in Korea. Flat river deltas produce the finest rice. Jeonju is considered the culinary capital of the peninsula. The region has a historical reputation for political marginalization by Seoul-centered power structures, producing a culture of independent thought and periodic resistance movements. The Donghak Peasant Revolution (1894) originated here.

**Economy:** Rice surplus production, seafood from the Yellow Sea coast, bamboo crafts, lacquerware, hanji (traditional paper) production.

**Notable:** Jeonju bibimbap, the most elaborate Korean regional cuisine. Gwangju is the major city of the south. The region produces a disproportionate number of artists, musicians and writers relative to its political influence.

**In modern times:** Jeonju is a major cultural tourism destination. Gwangju is known for the 1980 democratic uprising that shapes its modern identity. The region remains agriculturally productive and retains a distinct regional pride.

---

### Gyeongsang Province — 경상도

**Divided into:** North Gyeongsang (Gyeongbuk) and South Gyeongsang (Gyeongnam)

**Character:** The southeastern peninsula. Historically the heartland of the Silla dynasty. Gyeongju is the ancient capital with royal burial mounds and Buddhist temple complexes. The coast faces Japan across the Korea Strait. Busan is the primary port city and the main point of Japanese commercial and cultural contact in 1913. The region has a reputation for directness and pragmatism distinct from the formal Confucian culture of Chungcheong.

**Economy:** Coastal fishing, rice farming in river valleys, silk production, cotton weaving, port commerce at Busan.

**Notable:** Haeinsa temple houses the Tripitaka Koreana wooden printing blocks. Gyeongju's royal tombs are among the most important archaeological sites in East Asia. Japanese presence in Busan is substantial in 1913.

**In modern times:** Busan is Korea's second city. Gyeongbuk is home to major Korean conglomerate (chaebol) headquarters including Samsung's ancestral region. Gyeongju is a UNESCO heritage site.

---

### Gangwon Province — 강원도

**Character:** Mountainous eastern province bisected by the Taebaek mountain range. The spine of the range runs north-south, dropping to the East Sea (Sea of Japan) on the east and to broader valleys on the west. Sparse population, difficult terrain, cold winters. The region is culturally distinct from the lowland provinces with a tradition of independence and hardiness.

**Economy:** Highland farming (buckwheat, potatoes, millet), logging, mining (coal and iron deposits in northern areas), coastal fishing on the East Sea.

**Notable:** Seoraksan and other national mountain areas. Diamond Mountain (Geumgangsan) is in the northern part of the province, considered the most beautiful mountain in Korea and a major pilgrimage destination in 1913.

**In modern times:** The province is divided by the DMZ. South Gangwon contains ski resorts (Pyeongchang hosted the 2018 Winter Olympics). North Gangwon contains Geumgangsan, accessible to South Korean tourists only briefly in the early 2000s before the program ended.

---

### Hwanghae Province — 황해도

**Character:** Western province between the capital region and the northern provinces. Relatively flat coastal lowlands on the Yellow Sea. An agricultural transition zone. The port of Haeju is the major coastal town.

**Economy:** Rice and grain farming, coastal fishing, salt production from tidal flats, commerce along the Yellow Sea coast.

**In modern times:** Entirely within North Korea. Kaesong (Gaeseong in 1913) sits on the Hwanghae-Gyeonggi border and was the Goryeo dynasty capital, home to the finest ginseng in Korea. A joint Korean industrial zone operated at Kaesong from 2004 until 2016.

---

### Pyongan Province — 평안도

**Divided into:** North Pyongan (Pyeongbuk) and South Pyongan (Pyeongnam)

**Center:** Pyongyang, the second city of Korea and the largest city of the north

**Character:** The northern heartland. Pyongyang sits on the Taedong River and has been a major city since antiquity. The region has a reputation for commercial energy and a less rigid Confucian hierarchy than the capital region. Protestant Christianity spread rapidly here from the 1880s onward through American missionary activity; by 1913 Pyongyang has the highest concentration of Christians in Korea and is sometimes called the Jerusalem of the East. The region borders Manchuria and has significant Chinese cross-border trade.

**Economy:** Mining (coal, iron, copper, gold), ginseng trade through Gaeseong, livestock farming, cross-border commerce with Manchuria.

**Notable:** Pyongyang naengmyeon. Significant American Protestant missionary presence. The region's commercial culture produces a merchant class more prominent than in the south.

**In modern times:** Pyongyang is the capital of North Korea. The Christian community was destroyed under the Kim dynasty. The city has been entirely rebuilt in Soviet-influenced monumental style.

---

### Hamgyong Province — 함경도

**Divided into:** North Hamgyong (Hamgybuk) and South Hamgyong (Hamgynam)

**Character:** The extreme northeast. Borders Russia (a short stretch of the Tumen River) and Manchuria. The most mountainous and coldest part of Korea. Paektu Mountain (Baekdusan) on the Manchurian border is the sacred origin mountain in Korean cosmology and the highest point on the peninsula. The region is sparsely populated, frontier in character, with significant ethnic Korean communities across the border in Manchuria.

**Economy:** Mining, logging, fishing on the East Sea coast, livestock, cross-border trade with Manchuria and the Russian Maritime Province.

**Notable:** Paektu Mountain. The Tumen River border with Russia. A significant Korean diaspora lives across the border in Russia and Manchuria by 1913. The port of Wonsan (Wŏnsan) on the East Sea coast is a treaty port with foreign commercial presence.

**In modern times:** Entirely within North Korea. The region is the most isolated part of the peninsula. Hamhung is the second largest city of North Korea. The border region with China and Russia remains a zone of cross-border movement and trade in modern times.

## Social Structure 1913

### Yangban

The hereditary scholar-official class. Defined by Confucian education, success in the state examination system (gwageo), and ancestral lineage recorded in clan genealogies (jokbo). Yangban families own land, hold government posts and enforce formal social hierarchies. They do not engage in commerce or manual labor. Their cultural markers are Chinese literary education, formal dress (white silk or ramie robes, horsehair hats), calligraphy and ceremony.

In 1913 the formal examination system has collapsed with the modernizing reforms of the late 19th century and the Japanese pressure on the court. The yangban class retains social prestige and land ownership but is losing its exclusive claim to government positions.

### Jungin

Middle class of technical specialists. Translators, doctors, accountants, lower officials, artists. A hereditary status below yangban but above commoners. In 1913 this class is well-positioned to adapt to modernization, as technical skills are increasingly valued.

### Sangmin (Commoners)

Farmers, artisans, merchants. The bulk of the population. Farmers held the highest moral status among commoners in Confucian theory (agriculture as virtuous labor) but merchants were theoretically the lowest. In practice merchants could accumulate significant wealth.

### Baekjeong

Hereditary outcaste class performing butchering, leatherwork, executioner duties and other ritually polluting occupations. Legally abolished in the 1894 Gabo Reforms but social discrimination persists into 1913 and beyond.

## Religion 1913

**Buddhism** — Present since the 4th century CE. Suppressed under Joseon's Confucian state orthodoxy but surviving in mountain temples. Monks and nuns maintain temple communities. Popular Buddhism integrates with shamanistic practices. In 1913 a Buddhist revival is underway.

**Confucianism** — The state ideology and social operating system. Defines family hierarchy, gender roles, mourning rites, ancestor veneration and political theory. Not a religion in the Western sense but a comprehensive ethical system permeating all social relations.

**Shamanism (Musok)** — Indigenous Korean shamanic practice. Female shamans (mudang) perform gut ceremonies for healing, exorcism, divination and communication with ancestors. Practiced across all social classes despite Confucian disapproval. Robust in 1913 and surviving in modern times, particularly in rural areas and as a recognized intangible cultural heritage.

**Protestant Christianity** — Arrived with American missionaries in the 1880s. By 1913 deeply established in Pyongyang and spreading through the country. Associated with modernization, literacy campaigns (using the hangul alphabet) and Korean nationalist resistance to Japanese pressure. Major denominations: Presbyterian and Methodist.

**Catholic Christianity** — Present since the 18th century through Chinese contact. Subject to periodic persecution under Joseon. By 1913 established with a significant community, particularly in the southwest.

**Cheondogyo** — Korean new religion developed from the Donghak movement. Syncretic blend of Confucianism, Buddhism, Taoism and indigenous Korean belief with a strong nationalist and egalitarian social message. Significant following in 1913, particularly among commoners and the politically active.

## Language and Writing

**Korean language** — The national language. Agglutinative, verb-final, with extensive honorific register system. Speech style shifts dramatically based on social relationship between speakers: formal polite, informal polite, plain and intimate registers all differ significantly. Using the wrong register is a significant social offense.

**Hangul** — The Korean alphabet, invented in 1443 by King Sejong. In 1913 used for vernacular writing, women's correspondence, religious texts and newspapers targeting commoners. Educated yangban men still write in classical Chinese for formal documents.

**Classical Chinese (Hanja)** — The prestige written language of the educated class. Government documents, formal correspondence and elite literature are written in classical Chinese in 1913. Hangul is considered the people's script (언문, eonmun) and slightly lowbrow by conservative scholars.

In modern South Korea hangul is the primary writing system. Hanja appears in newspapers, legal documents and academic contexts as supplementary notation.

## Key Cultural Practices

**Ancestor Veneration (Jesa)** — Ritual ceremonies performed on death anniversaries and major holidays to honor deceased family members. A full table of ritual foods is laid out, incense burned, bows performed. The most important family obligation in 1913 and still widely practiced in modern Korea.

**Hanbok** — Traditional Korean clothing. White for everyday wear; colored silk for festive occasions. Men wear jeogori jacket and baji trousers with a topknot (sangtu) and gat hat. Women wear a short jeogori jacket and full chima skirt. Class and status visible through fabric quality and color. In 1913 hanbok is everyday dress. In modern times worn for holidays, weddings and formal occasions.

**Ondol Heating** — Radiant floor heating system. Wood fires under stone floors heat the room from below. Koreans sit and sleep on the floor on cushions and mats (yo). This system shapes interior design and social behavior (floor-level furniture, cushion seating). In modern times ondol principles are replicated with hot water pipes under modern floors (hydronic radiant heating).

**Kimjang** — Autumn communal kimchi-making. Families and neighborhoods gather to prepare large quantities of kimchi before winter. Community labor, shared ingredients, reciprocal help. A UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage in modern times. Unchanged in social character from 1913.
