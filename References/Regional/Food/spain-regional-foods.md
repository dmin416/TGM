# Spain — Regional Foods and Drinks

## Andalusia

### Foods
1. **Gazpacho** — A cold, refreshing soup made from raw tomatoes, cucumbers, green peppers, garlic, olive oil, vinegar and bread, all blended until smooth. The bread provides body and richness while the vegetables give freshness. Served ice-cold in summer heat, often garnished with diced vegetables, hard-boiled egg or croutons. The flavor is bright, tangy and intensely vegetable-forward. Every family has their own proportions, with some versions quite garlicky and others emphasizing tomato sweetness. Originated as peasant food but now celebrated worldwide.

2. **Jamón ibérico** — The crown jewel of Spanish charcuterie, made from black Iberian pigs that roam oak forests eating acorns (bellotas) during the montanera season. The acorn diet creates marble-white fat streaks throughout deep red meat, with a distinctive nutty, sweet flavor. The hams are salt-cured and aged 24-48 months in cool, dry cellars. The texture is silky and melting, dissolving on the tongue. Jamón ibérico de bellota represents the highest grade. Cut paper-thin by hand using long, flexible knives. Serve at room temperature with manchego cheese or simply alone.

3. **Pescaíto frito** — An assortment of tiny whole fish and seafood including anchovies, squid, red mullet, sole and whitebait, all lightly dusted in wheat or chickpea flour and deep-fried in abundant olive oil until golden and crispy. The result is crunchy outside, tender inside, with intense ocean flavor. Eaten hot from paper cones at beach chiringuitos (beach bars) or tapas bars. Squeeze lemon over top and eat whole, bones and all. The skill is in achieving perfect crispness without greasiness. A defining taste of coastal Andalusia.

4. **Salmorejo** — A thick, creamy cold soup from Córdoba made with tomatoes, bread, garlic, olive oil and vinegar, blended until smooth and velvety. Much thicker than gazpacho, with a texture like a savory smoothie. Traditionally garnished with diced jamón serrano and chopped hard-boiled egg on top. The bread-to-tomato ratio is higher than gazpacho, creating body and richness. Served in small bowls or glasses as a substantial tapa. The flavor is mellow and tomatoey with background garlic notes. Best made with ripe summer tomatoes.

5. **Rabo de toro** — A rich, deeply flavored oxtail stew that is Córdoba's signature dish, historically made with the tails of bulls killed in the bullring. The oxtail is slowly braised for hours in red wine with carrots, onions, tomatoes and bay leaves until the meat falls off the bone and the sauce reduces to thick, glossy richness. The collagen from the tails creates silky, unctuous texture. The flavor is profoundly beefy and wine-dark. Served with fried potatoes or crusty bread to soak up sauce. A dish of incredible depth.

6. **Churros con chocolate** — Long, ridged sticks of deep-fried choux pastry dusted with sugar, served with a cup of intensely thick, dark hot chocolate for dipping. The churros are crispy outside, light and airy inside with a slightly chewy texture. The chocolate is made with cornstarch to thicken it to pudding-like consistency, rich and barely sweet. Eaten for breakfast or as an afternoon snack, especially after late nights out. The combination of crispy, sweet churro and bitter-sweet chocolate is addictive. Best fresh from specialist churrerías.

7. **Tortilla de camarones** — Paper-thin fritters from Cádiz made with tiny shrimp, chickpea flour, onion and parsley, deep-fried until crispy and lacy. The texture is delicate and crunchy, shattering at first bite. The tiny shrimp (camarones) are so small they're eaten whole, adding bursts of briny flavor throughout. The chickpea flour provides nutty notes and creates the characteristic crispy texture. Served hot as a tapa with sherry or beer. Difficult to make well, requiring skill to achieve the proper thinness and crispness.

8. **Flamenquín** — A specialty of Córdoba consisting of a thin slice of pork loin wrapped around jamón serrano, rolled tightly, breaded and deep-fried until golden. Sometimes cheese is added to the filling. Cut into thick slices revealing the spiral of pink meat inside. The exterior is crunchy while the interior remains juicy, with the salty ham providing contrast to mild pork. Served hot with french fries and sometimes aioli. The name supposedly comes from Flemish (flamenco) soldiers who introduced similar preparations.

9. **Pringá** — A rustic, intensely flavored meat spread made from the leftover meats and chickpeas from cooking cocido (a traditional stew). The meats (usually pork, beef, chicken, chorizo and morcilla) are shredded and mashed together with some of the cooking fat and chickpeas until spreadable. The result is rich, fatty and deeply savory. Traditionally eaten in thick sandwiches called pringá montaditos, especially popular after late nights. Every family's pringá tastes different depending on which meats were used. Pure comfort food.

10. **Polvorones** — Crumbly, melt-in-the-mouth cookies made with flour, almonds, sugar and lard, traditionally eaten at Christmas. The dough is toasted before mixing, giving a distinctive toasted flour flavor. The texture is extremely delicate and powdery (polvo means dust), literally crumbling to powder in your mouth. Wrapped individually in paper that must be carefully unwound. The challenge is eating one without it falling apart. Flavored with cinnamon or lemon zest. Made throughout Spain but especially associated with Estepa in Seville province.

### Drinks
- **Sherry** — Fortified wine from the Jerez region made from Palomino grapes using the solera aging system. Fino is bone-dry, pale and delicate, best served chilled. Manzanilla is similar but from Sanlúcar de Barrameda, with salty notes from sea air. Oloroso is darker, richer, nutty and intense. Pedro Ximénez is thick, sweet, almost black, made from sun-dried grapes, tasting of raisins and figs. Amontillado falls between fino and oloroso. Each style is distinct, ranging from aperitif to dessert wine.
- **Tinto de verano** — Red wine mixed with lemon or orange soda (typically Fanta or La Casera), served over ice. Lighter and more refreshing than sangria, less sweet. The citrus soda cuts the wine's heaviness, creating a thirst-quenching summer drink. Ratio is roughly half wine, half soda, but varies by preference. Found at every bar and beach chiringuito. The working-class alternative to sangria. Best with simple red wine, nothing fancy. Garnished with lemon slice.
- **Rebujito** — Sherry (fino or manzanilla) mixed with lemon-lime soda (usually 7-Up or Sprite), served ice-cold. The traditional drink of Seville's April Fair (Feria de Abril), consumed in vast quantities during the week-long festival. Light, refreshing, dangerously easy to drink. The sherry's dryness balances the soda's sweetness. Typically 50/50 ratio. Served in small glasses or large pitchers. The ultimate Andalusian party drink. Deceptively strong despite tasting light and sweet.

## Catalonia

### Foods
1. **Pa amb tomàquet** — The foundation of Catalan cuisine: thick slices of rustic bread rubbed with the cut side of ripe tomatoes until the bread absorbs the juice and pulp, then drizzled with extra virgin olive oil and sprinkled with coarse salt. The bread becomes pink-red and juicy, with fresh tomato flavor enhanced by fruity oil and salt. Simple but transformative. Variations include toasting the bread first. Served as a base for toppings like jamón, cheese or anchovies, or eaten alone. Every Catalan table has this. The quality of ingredients is everything here.

2. **Crema catalana** — Catalonia's answer to crème brûlée, but lighter and flavored with cinnamon and lemon or orange zest rather than vanilla. A silky custard made with milk (not cream), egg yolks, sugar and cornstarch, cooked until thick then chilled. Just before serving, sugar is sprinkled on top and caramelized with a hot iron or torch to create a crisp, glassy crust that shatters at the tap of a spoon. The contrast of cold, creamy custard with warm, brittle caramel is essential. Traditionally served on Saint Joseph's Day (March 19).

3. **Escalivada** — Smoky, sweet roasted vegetables—red peppers, eggplant, onions and sometimes tomatoes—cooked directly over flames or hot coals until the skins char and blister. The vegetables are peeled, torn into strips and dressed with olive oil, garlic and salt. The fire adds deep, smoky sweetness. Served at room temperature as a salad, tapa, or on bread. The key is roasting until completely soft and sweet, with charred flavor throughout. Often served with anchovies or salt cod. Embodies rustic Catalan cooking.

4. **Fideuà** — A paella-like dish from Valencia/Catalonia using short, thin pasta (fideos) instead of rice, cooked in shallow pans with seafood stock, squid, shrimp and fish until the pasta absorbs the liquid and develops a toasted bottom crust (socarrat). The pasta strands should be slightly al dente, infused with seafood flavor. Finished with aioli stirred through. Traditionally made by fishermen using broken pasta pieces. The contrast of tender pasta and crispy bottom is essential. Served directly from the pan for sharing.

5. **Botifarra amb mongetes** — Grilled botifarra sausage (coarse pork sausage seasoned with salt, pepper and spices) served with white beans (mongetes) that have been stewed with olive oil, garlic and herbs. The beans are creamy and tender, their mild flavor contrasting with the savory, slightly fatty sausage. The sausage may be botifarra blanca (white) or negra (black, with blood). Simple but satisfying. The beans must be cooked until completely tender but still holding their shape. A classic combination found throughout Catalonia.

6. **Calçots con romesco** — Fat spring onions (calçots) grilled over open flames until charred on the outside and tender inside, served with romesco sauce for dipping. The charred outer layers are peeled away, revealing sweet, tender inner onions. Romesco is a complex sauce made from roasted tomatoes, red peppers, almonds, hazelnuts, garlic, olive oil and dried ñora peppers, ground to a thick paste. Eaten at calçotades (calçot feasts) from late winter to spring. Messy, communal eating involving bibs and lots of wine.

7. **Esqueixada** — A refreshing salad of shredded salt cod (bacalao) that has been desalted and torn into thin strips, tossed with tomatoes, onions, black olives, olive oil and vinegar. No cooking involved—the cod is eaten raw after soaking. The texture is firm and slightly chewy, the flavor mild and clean. The vegetables provide freshness and crunch. Served cold as a summer dish. The name means "torn" in Catalan, referring to how the cod is prepared. Essential tapa in Barcelona.

8. **Mel i mató** — The simplest of desserts: fresh, unsalted mató cheese (similar to ricotta) drizzled with honey and optionally sprinkled with walnuts. The cheese is soft, mild and slightly grainy, made from cow or goat milk whey. The honey adds sweetness and floral notes. The combination is ancient, pure and satisfying. The quality of honey matters enormously—typically using local Catalan honey. Light and refreshing after heavy meals. Often served in the cheese's traditional woven basket.

9. **Xuixo** — A specialty of Girona, these are croissant-like pastries deep-fried until golden, filled with thick crema catalana (custard), then rolled in sugar. The exterior is crispy and sweet, the interior rich, creamy and custard-filled. Eaten warm when the custard is still flowing. The dough is laminated like puff pastry, creating layers that fry up crisp. Dangerously addictive. The name comes from Catalan slang meaning "kiss." Best from traditional bakeries in Girona.

10. **Suquet de peix** — A traditional Catalan fisherman's stew made with firm white fish (monkfish, grouper, sea bass), potatoes, tomatoes, garlic, saffron and a picada (a ground mixture of toasted bread, almonds, garlic and parsley) that thickens and enriches the broth. The result is a thick, intensely flavored sauce coating tender fish and soft potatoes. Served in terracotta dishes. The picada adds body and nutty complexity. Coastal villages have their own versions. Meant to use the catch of the day.

### Drinks
- **Cava** — Spanish sparkling wine made using the traditional method (like Champagne), primarily from the Penedès region near Barcelona. Made mainly from Macabeo, Xarel·lo and Parellada grapes. Ranges from brut nature (bone dry) to dulce (sweet). The best cavas have fine bubbles, crisp acidity, apple and citrus flavors, sometimes toasty notes from aging. Quality varies enormously from cheap supermarket bottles to world-class wines. Essential for celebrations but also everyday drinking. Freixenet and Codorníu are largest producers. Smaller boutique cavas can rival Champagne.
- **Priorat red wine** — Intense, powerful red wines from the Priorat region, made primarily from old-vine Garnacha and Cariñena grown in steep hillside vineyards with licorella (slate) soil. Dark, concentrated and full-bodied with black fruit, mineral notes and firm tannins. Among Spain's finest and most expensive wines. The slate soil gives distinctive mineral character. Requires aging to soften. Pairs with robust meat dishes. The region was nearly abandoned until revival in 1980s-90s. Now cult wines.
- **Ratafia** — Traditional Catalan herbal liqueur made by macerating green walnuts, herbs and spices in clear spirit, then sweetening. Dark brown color, complex bitter-sweet flavor with walnut, herb and spice notes. Served as a digestif after meals. Every monastery, pharmacy and family had their own recipe historically. Commercial versions now standardized. The green walnuts are picked on St. John's Eve (June 23). Similar to Italian nocino. Powerful and aromatic.

## Basque Country

### Foods
1. **Pintxos** — The Basque version of tapas, small bites served on slices of bread and held together with toothpicks. Unlike tapas which are served on plates, pintxos are displayed on bar counters for customers to help themselves. The variety is staggering: from simple jamón on bread to elaborate constructions with seafood, peppers, anchovies, tortilla, croquettes. The name comes from the toothpick (pincho) that spears them. San Sebastián's Old Town has legendary pintxo bars where locals bar-hop, eating one or two at each stop. Part art, part competition between bars.

2. **Bacalao al pil-pil** — A legendary Basque dish of salt cod cooked gently in olive oil with garlic and dried chili, creating an emulsified sauce through the cod's own gelatin and constant shaking of the pan. The result is thick, glossy, pale green sauce coating tender flakes of cod. No other ingredients—just cod, oil, garlic, chili and patience. The pil-pil refers to the bubbling sound of the sauce emulsifying. Requires skill and practice to achieve proper emulsion. Served in terracotta dishes with bread for dipping. Pure technique.

3. **Marmitako** — A hearty Basque fisherman's stew traditionally made with tuna (or bonito), potatoes, onions, peppers, tomatoes and pimentón. The tuna is added at the end to prevent overcooking, remaining tender and flaking into large chunks. The broth is thick with potatoes that break down slightly, creating body. Named after the marmita (pot) fishermen used on boats. The flavor is rich and satisfying, the tuna providing meaty substance. Best made with fresh bonito during its season. Comfort food of the highest order.

4. **Txangurro** — Spider crab meat sautéed with onion, tomato, garlic and brandy, then stuffed back into the cleaned crab shell, topped with breadcrumbs and briefly broiled until golden. The crab meat is sweet and delicate, the sauce rich and slightly spicy. The presentation in the shell is dramatic and traditional. Eaten with a small fork, scooping meat and sauce. San Sebastián specialty. The brandy adds depth and slight sweetness. Labor-intensive to prepare but spectacular results.

5. **Idiazabal cheese** — A firm, lightly smoked sheep's milk cheese from the mountains of Basque Country with protected designation. The smoking is done over beech or cherry wood, giving distinctive amber color and subtle smoky flavor without overwhelming the sweet, nutty taste of the sheep's milk. The texture is firm but not hard, slightly oily. Aged minimum two months. Excellent for eating plain, grated over vegetables, or with quince paste and walnuts. The smoking tradition comes from shepherds who stored cheese near their fire.

6. **Kokotxas** — The gelatinous throat pieces (kokotxas) of hake or cod, considered a delicacy for their unique texture and ability to create naturally emulsified sauces. Usually cooked al pil-pil style in olive oil with garlic until they release their gelatin and create a thick, glossy sauce. The texture is tender, almost like softshell crab or bone marrow, melting on the tongue. Expensive and prized. Found in high-end Basque restaurants. An acquired taste but beloved by locals. The sauce is addictive when done properly.

7. **Gilda** — The simplest and most iconic pintxo: a skewer of anchovy, green olive and pickled guindilla pepper. The combination of salty anchovy, briny olive and spicy-sour pepper is perfectly balanced. Named after Rita Hayworth's character in the film Gilda, supposedly because it's "salty, spicy and leaves you wanting more." Found in every Basque bar. The quality of each ingredient matters. Often the first pintxo eaten when starting a bar crawl. Deceptively simple but archetypal.

8. **Piperrada** — Scrambled eggs cooked with sautéed red and green peppers, onions, tomatoes and sometimes jamón or chorizo. The vegetables are cooked slowly until soft and sweet, then eggs are stirred in until just set and creamy. The result is colorful, sweet and savory. Eaten for breakfast or as a light meal. The name comes from piper (pepper in Basque). Every household has their own version. Sometimes served as a sauce for fish or meat. The peppers must be soft and almost melting.

9. **Talo** — Traditional Basque corn flatbread cooked on a griddle, similar to a thick tortilla. Made simply from cornmeal, water and salt, worked into a soft dough and shaped into rounds. Cooked on both sides until lightly charred. Eaten hot, often filled with chorizo, cheese or txistorra (Basque sausage). The texture is tender and slightly chewy with corn sweetness. Ancient food that sustained shepherds and farmers. Still made at traditional cider houses. Best fresh from the griddle.

10. **Pantxineta** — A Basque pastry made from puff pastry filled with thick almond cream (frangipane), folded into a half-moon shape and baked until golden and crispy. The almond filling is dense, sweet and marzipan-like. Sometimes topped with powdered sugar or sliced almonds. The layers of pastry shatter delicately, contrasting with the rich filling. Traditionally eaten on All Saints' Day but available year-round in pastry shops. Excellent with coffee. The name may come from the belly (pantxa) because of its rounded shape.

### Drinks
- **Txakoli** — Lightly sparkling, very dry white wine from the Basque coast, made primarily from Hondarrabi Zuri grapes. High acidity, low alcohol, with green apple and citrus flavors. Traditionally poured from height to aerate and create bubbles. The signature wine of Basque pintxo bars. Pronounced "chah-koh-lee." Best drunk young and cold. The slight spritz refreshes the palate between pintxos. Most production around Getaria. Some rosé versions exist. Essential pairing for Basque seafood and pintxos. Refreshing and food-friendly.
- **Basque cider (sagardoa)** — Natural, still cider made from local apple varieties, tart and funky with barnyard notes. Low alcohol, completely dry, cloudy appearance. Drunk at sidrerías where massive barrels line the walls. The ritual: barrels are opened with a shout of "txotx!" and cider streams out. Drinkers catch it in their glasses held low, then drink immediately in one gulp. Only a finger of cider poured at a time. The cider season runs January to April. Pairs with cod omelets and grilled steak. An experience as much as a drink.
- **Patxaran** — Basque sloe berry liqueur made by steeping sloe berries (from blackthorn bushes) in anise-flavored spirit with coffee beans and vanilla, then sweetening. Deep red-purple color, sweet with berry and anise flavors. Served as a digestif, often ice-cold. Traditional after heavy meals. The sloes are harvested in late autumn. Every family has their recipe, jealously guarded. Commercial versions available but homemade is prized. Also spelled pacharán. Smooth and warming despite the cold serving temperature.

## Galicia

### Foods
1. **Pulpo a la gallega** — Octopus tentacles boiled until tender, sliced into thick rounds and served on wooden plates, drizzled with olive oil and sprinkled with coarse salt and smoked paprika (pimentón). The cooking technique is crucial: the octopus is dipped in boiling water three times before being fully submerged, which helps tenderize it. Traditionally cooked in large copper pots. The result is tender, slightly chewy meat with smoky, spicy, salty coating. Essential tapa at Galician festivals. Served with boiled potatoes. Also called pulpo á feira (fair-style octopus).

2. **Empanada gallega** — Large, flat savory pies with a thick bread-like crust enclosing fillings of tuna (atún), pork loin (lomo), cod (bacalao) or other ingredients with sofrito (onions, peppers, tomatoes). The dough is made with wheat or corn flour, enriched with lard or oil. Baked until golden, the crust is sturdy enough to hold while eating. Cut into squares and eaten hot or cold. Every bakery makes them. The filling should be moist but not wet, well-seasoned with paprika. Traditional festival and celebration food.

3. **Lacón con grelos** — A rustic dish of cured pork shoulder (lacón) boiled with turnip greens (grelos), potatoes and chorizo. The pork is salty and slightly fatty, the greens are bitter and tender, the potatoes absorb the flavorful cooking liquid. Often a few white beans are added. The combination might sound strange but is perfectly balanced. Traditionally eaten during Carnival before Lent. The grelos are a winter green, available only a few months. Comfort food that sticks to your ribs.

4. **Percebes** — Goose barnacles, strange-looking crustaceans that grow on wave-battered rocks and are harvested at great risk by percebeiros (barnacle collectors). They look like miniature dragon claws. Simply boiled in seawater for seconds, the tough outer skin is peeled away to reveal sweet, tender meat with intense ocean flavor. Expensive and prized. The taste is clean, briny and slightly metallic—pure essence of the sea. Best eaten immediately while hot. Accompany with Albariño wine.

5. **Caldo gallego** — A hearty soup of white beans, turnip greens (grelos), potatoes and pork (usually salted pork shoulder or ribs). The broth is rich and slightly fatty from the pork, the beans are creamy, the greens add slight bitterness and the potatoes provide body. Simmered for hours until everything is tender. Every grandmother has her recipe. Served steaming hot with crusty bread. Essential winter food that warms from inside. The secret is slow cooking and good quality pork.

6. **Tarta de Santiago** — A dense, moist almond cake from Santiago de Compostela, made simply with ground almonds, sugar, eggs and lemon zest, with no flour. Baked until the top forms a golden crust. Before serving, a paper cross of St. James is placed on top, powdered sugar is dusted over, then the cross is removed, leaving the white cross shape on the golden cake. The texture is like marzipan turned into cake—rich, nutty and not too sweet. Traditional dessert for pilgrims completing the Camino de Santiago.

7. **Vieiras** — Scallops from the cold Galician waters, particularly prized for their size and sweet flavor. Traditionally cooked in their own shells over open flames with breadcrumbs, garlic, olive oil and parsley, or simply grilled with a squeeze of lemon. The meat is tender, sweet and slightly briny. Vieiras (scallop shells) are the symbol of the Camino de Santiago pilgrimage route. Fresh Galician scallops are incomparable. Often served as a tapa or first course.

8. **Pimientos de Padrón** — Small green peppers from the town of Padrón, fried whole in olive oil until blistered and charred, then sprinkled with coarse sea salt. Most are mild and sweet, but about one in ten is fiercely hot—creating a culinary Russian roulette. Eaten as a tapa, holding the stem and eating the whole pepper in one or two bites. The saying goes: "Os pementos de Padrón, uns pican e outros non" (Padrón peppers, some are hot and some are not). Summer specialty.

9. **Tetilla cheese** — A soft, creamy cow's milk cheese shaped like a small breast (tetilla means teat), with a thin, golden rind. The texture is smooth and buttery, the flavor mild, milky and slightly tangy when young, developing nuttiness with age. Made throughout Galicia with protected designation. Melts beautifully. Excellent for eating plain with bread or membrillo (quince paste). The shape is traditional and distinctive. One of Spain's most popular cheeses.

10. **Merluza a la gallega** — Hake, Galicia's most prized fish, poached simply and served with boiled potatoes, drizzled with olive oil and sprinkled with paprika and garlic. The preparation is deliberately simple to showcase the fish's delicate, sweet flavor and tender, flaky texture. Sometimes a few drops of vinegar are added. The key is absolute freshness and careful cooking to prevent the fish from becoming tough. Galician hake is considered the finest in the world. Essential dish in any Galician restaurant.

### Drinks
- **Albariño** — Crisp, aromatic white wine made from Albariño grapes grown in the Rías Baixas region of coastal Galicia. Bright acidity, stone fruit and citrus flavors, sometimes peach or apricot notes, with distinctive saline minerality from Atlantic influence. Light to medium body. The perfect wine for Galician seafood, especially oysters and octopus. Among Spain's finest white wines. Must be drunk young, within 2-3 years. The salty edge comes from vineyards near the ocean. Refreshing and elegant. Best served well-chilled.
- **Orujo** — Clear grape pomace brandy, the Galician version of grappa. Made from the skins, seeds and stems left over from winemaking. Harsh and fiery when young, smooth when aged. Typical alcohol content 40-50%. Served as a digestif after big meals. Often flavored with herbs (hierbas), coffee (café), or cream (crema de orujo). The cream version is sweet and popular. Traditionally homemade illegally but now commercial production regulated. Burns going down but warms from within. The hangover remedy that causes hangovers.
- **Queimada** — Flaming ritual drink made by setting orujo on fire in a clay pot with sugar, lemon peel, coffee beans and sometimes herbs. The fire burns off alcohol while caramelizing sugar. Served after the flames die down, still hot. Traditional at festivals and gatherings, accompanied by a chanted spell in Galician to ward off evil spirits and witches. The ceremony is part of the experience. The resulting drink is sweet, smoky and warming. More about tradition and spectacle than taste. Tourist attraction but also genuine folk tradition.

## Valencia

### Foods
1. **Paella valenciana** — The original paella from Valencia, cooked in a wide, shallow pan over open flames using bomba or calasparra rice with chicken, rabbit, garrafón beans (large white lima beans), tavella beans (green beans), tomatoes, olive oil and saffron. The rice absorbs the flavorful stock and develops a prized crispy bottom layer called socarrat. No seafood in authentic Valencian paella. The dish should be slightly wet, not dry, with rice al dente. Traditionally eaten directly from the pan using wooden spoons. Originally farmer and worker food cooked over orange wood fires in the fields.

2. **Arroz al horno** — Baked rice cooked in an earthenware casserole with chickpeas, pork ribs, morcilla (blood sausage), potatoes, tomatoes and garlic. The dish is assembled then baked in the oven until the rice is tender and the top develops a golden crust. The chickpeas become creamy, the pork renders fat into the rice. Served directly in the terracotta dish. Every family has variations. The rice should be loose, not soupy or too dry. Hearty, rustic and deeply satisfying. Traditional Sunday lunch in Valencia.

3. **Fideuà** — Similar to paella but using thin, short noodles (fideos) instead of rice, cooked in fish stock with squid, shrimp, monkfish and other seafood. The noodles toast slightly in olive oil before the stock is added, creating depth of flavor. Cooked in wide, shallow pans until the liquid is absorbed. The noodles should have slight bite and be infused with seafood flavor. Traditionally topped with aioli stirred through at the table. Originated in fishing towns as an alternative to rice. The socarrat (crusty bottom) is equally prized here.

4. **Horchata** — A sweet, milky-white drink made from pressed tiger nuts (chufas), water and sugar. The tiger nuts are small tubers with a distinctive sweet, nutty flavor. The horchata is served ice-cold, slightly thick and creamy in texture, tasting of almonds, vanilla and earth. Completely refreshing in summer heat. Drunk throughout Spain but originally from Valencia where the tiger nuts are grown. Best fresh from horchaterías where it's made daily. Sometimes topped with cinnamon. Non-dairy despite the creamy appearance.

5. **Fartons** — Long, thin, lightly sweetened bread rolls with a soft, fluffy texture and glazed surface. Designed specifically for dunking in horchata, their shape perfect for dipping. The dough is slightly sweet but not overly so, enriched with oil and sugar, topped with a simple sugar glaze. When fresh, they're impossibly light and airy. Eaten as breakfast or a snack. The combination of cold horchata and sweet, soft farton is Valencia's classic pairing. Best from traditional bakeries.

6. **All i pebre** — A traditional eel stew made with eels, garlic, paprika, olive oil and sometimes potatoes, creating a thick, rusty-red sauce. The eels are cut into segments and cooked until tender in the garlicky, spicy sauce. The name means garlic and pepper in Valencian. The flavor is intense—garlicky, earthy from the eels, with background heat from paprika. Served in terracotta pots with bread for soaking up sauce. An acquired taste but beloved locally. Traditional fisher folk food from the Albufera lake.

7. **Bunyols** — Light, airy fritters made from a simple batter of flour, eggs, milk and mashed pumpkin or sweet potato, deep-fried until golden and puffy, then dusted with sugar. The pumpkin version is traditional for All Saints' Day and Las Fallas festival. The fritters are crispy outside, soft and slightly sweet inside with subtle pumpkin flavor. Best eaten hot and fresh. Found at festivals and bakeries. Some versions are plain (bunyols de vent—wind fritters) that puff up like air. Addictive when fresh.

8. **Arros negre** — Black rice cooked with cuttlefish or squid ink, giving it a dramatic black color and deep, briny seafood flavor. The rice is cooked in fish stock with cuttlefish, squid, garlic, tomatoes and ink sacs. The result is tender rice with intense ocean flavor and slightly chewy pieces of seafood throughout. Served with aioli on the side. The black color comes entirely from natural ink. The flavor is much more complex than it appears. Popular along the Mediterranean coast.

9. **Esgarraet** — A simple salad of shredded salt cod that's been desalted, mixed with roasted red peppers that have been peeled and torn into strips, dressed with olive oil and garlic. Sometimes tuna or black olives are added. The salt cod provides firm texture and mild fishy flavor, the peppers add sweetness and smokiness. Served at room temperature. The name comes from the Valencian verb meaning "to tear." Essential tapa in Valencia. Best with crusty bread to soak up the garlicky oil.

10. **Turrón** — Nougat confection made with honey, sugar, egg whites and toasted almonds, pressed into bars and cut into slices. Two main types: turrón de Jijona (soft, ground to a paste) and turrón de Alicante (hard, with whole almonds visible). Both are intensely sweet with toasted almond flavor. Traditional Christmas treat but eaten year-round. The hard version requires strong teeth and breaks into shards. The soft version is like almond butter candy. Jijona and Alicante in Valencia region are the traditional production centers.

### Drinks
- **Agua de Valencia** — A potent cocktail invented in 1959 at Café Madrid in Valencia, combining cava (Spanish sparkling wine), orange juice, vodka and gin, shaken together and served in large glasses or pitchers. Deceptively easy to drink despite high alcohol content. Tastes like sophisticated mimosa but packs serious punch. Best with fresh-squeezed Valencian orange juice. Popular brunch drink. The sweetness hides the spirits. Often causes unexpected hangovers. Served ice-cold. Refreshing and dangerous in equal measure.
- **Mistela** — Sweet fortified wine made by adding grape spirit to fresh grape must before fermentation, preserving natural grape sugars. Golden to amber color, very sweet with intense grape and sometimes floral notes. Served cold as aperitif or with desserts. Similar to port or sweet sherry but distinctly Spanish. Made throughout Valencia and Catalonia. Some versions aged in barrels develop more complexity. The sweetness comes entirely from grapes, not added sugar. Excellent with blue cheese or almonds.
- **Valencian wine** — The Utiel-Requena region inland from Valencia produces robust red wines primarily from Bobal grapes, an indigenous variety. The wines are full-bodied, dark in color, with red and black fruit flavors and firm tannins. Good value for money. Bobal was historically underrated but now gaining recognition. Some modern producers make excellent wines. The region also produces rosés and cavas. The continental climate gives wines structure. Pairs well with paella and rice dishes.

## Castile (Madrid region)

### Foods
1. **Cocido madrileño** — Madrid's emblematic dish, a substantial chickpea stew with various meats (beef, pork, chicken, chorizo, morcilla), vegetables (cabbage, carrots, potatoes) and bones for flavor, all simmered together for hours. Traditionally served in three courses: first the broth with noodles, then the chickpeas and vegetables, finally the meats. The broth is clear and deeply flavored, the chickpeas tender and creamy, the meats falling apart. A complete meal. Every family has their recipe. Essential winter food. Takes all day to make properly.

2. **Bocadillo de calamares** — A Madrid institution: a crusty bread roll filled with fried squid rings. The squid is coated in light batter or flour and fried until golden and tender. Served simply in bread, sometimes with a squeeze of lemon but often plain. The contrast of crispy squid and soft bread is perfect. Found at bars around Plaza Mayor where people eat them standing at counters. The squid should be tender, not rubbery. Simple but addictive. Often eaten after late nights out.

3. **Huevos rotos** — Literally "broken eggs"—fried eggs with runny yolks served over a mountain of fried potatoes and often topped with jamón serrano or chorizo. The yolks are deliberately broken so they run over the potatoes. The key is cooking the eggs just right so the whites are crispy at the edges but the yolks remain liquid. The fried potatoes are cut thick and fried until golden. When mixed together, the yolk becomes sauce for the potatoes. Rich, simple and satisfying.

4. **Sopa de ajo** — Garlic soup made from stale bread, garlic, paprika, olive oil and water or stock, with a poached egg on top. The bread breaks down to thicken the soup. The garlic is fried in olive oil until golden, releasing its sweetness. Paprika adds depth and color. The egg is poached directly in the soup. The result is rustic, warming and intensely garlicky. Peasant food elevated. Often baked in terracotta bowls. Perfect for cold days or hangovers.

5. **Judiones de La Granja** — Large, flat white beans (judiones) from the town of La Granja in Segovia, stewed slowly with pork ear, blood sausage, bacon and chorizo. The beans become creamy inside while holding their shape. The pork products add rich, fatty, smoky flavor. Simmered until the sauce is thick and the meat tender. Often finished with fried garlic. Hearty mountain food. The beans are prized and protected. Stick-to-your-ribs satisfying.

6. **Rosquillas** — Ring-shaped cookies traditionally eaten during Madrid's San Isidro festival in May. Four main varieties: tontas (plain), listas (glazed with sugar), de Santa Clara (with egg white meringue topping), and francesas (with lemon icing). The dough is lightly sweetened, enriched with olive oil and anise, giving subtle licorice notes. The texture is tender and crumbly. Found in pastry shops throughout Madrid but essential during festival time. Each type has devoted fans.

7. **Torrijas** — Spanish-style French toast made with thick slices of day-old bread soaked in milk infused with cinnamon and lemon peel, then dipped in beaten egg and fried in olive oil until golden. Drizzled with honey or dusted with cinnamon sugar. The result is crispy outside, custardy inside, sweet and aromatic. Traditional Easter and Lent dessert but eaten year-round. Some versions use wine instead of milk. Best eaten warm. Every grandmother has her method.

8. **Bartolillos** — Small, triangular fried pastries filled with thick, sweet custard cream, dusted with powdered sugar. The pastry dough is thin and crispy when fried, shattering delicately. The custard filling is rich, vanilla-flavored and still slightly warm when fresh. Traditional Madrid pastry, especially popular during San Isidro festival. Best from old bakeries that make them fresh daily. The custard should be thick enough not to leak out but creamy enough to flow slightly when bitten.

9. **Oreja a la plancha** — Grilled pig's ear, cooked until crispy on the outside and tender inside. The ear is boiled first until soft, then grilled until the edges crisp up. The texture is unique—gelatinous and slightly chewy with crispy bits. Seasoned simply with salt, sometimes served with lemon. Popular tapa in Madrid. Working-class food that makes use of every part of the animal. The collagen gives it rich mouthfeel. Often served with beer. An acquired taste that locals love.

### Drinks
- **Madrid wine** — The Vinos de Madrid designation covers wines from the areas surrounding the capital, including three sub-zones. Primarily red wines from Tempranillo (locally called Tinto Fino), also Garnacha. Generally medium-bodied, fruity, food-friendly wines for everyday drinking. Not Spain's most prestigious wines but honest and affordable. The Arganda sub-zone produces lighter reds, San Martín fuller-bodied wines. White wines also made but less common. Best enjoyed young. Perfect house wines for tapas bars.
- **Vermut** — Spanish-style vermouth, sweet and red, traditionally drunk as an aperitif before lunch, especially on Sundays. Served on ice with orange slice and sometimes an olive. The Spanish vermouth culture is distinct, with dedicated vermuterías. Homemade versions infused with herbs were traditional. Commercial brands like Yzaguirre and Martini dominate now. The ritual of "ir de vermut" (going for vermouth) involves the drink plus olives, chips and socializing. The sweet, herbal, slightly bitter flavor prepares the appetite.
- **Clara** — Beer mixed with lemon soda (typically Fanta Limón or La Casera), Spain's refreshing beer cocktail. Usually half beer, half lemon soda, served very cold. Called clara de limón specifically. Some prefer gaseosa (a milder lemon soda) for less sweetness. The ultimate summer refreshment, quenching thirst better than plain beer. Low alcohol, highly drinkable. Found at every bar and beach. Variations include clara con casera. The working person's summer drink. Light, bubbly and refreshing.

## Asturias

### Foods
1. **Fabada asturiana** — Asturias's most famous dish, a rich white bean stew made with large fabes (butter beans), chorizo, morcilla (blood sausage), pork shoulder or bacon, and saffron. The beans are cooked slowly until creamy but still holding their shape, the pork products releasing fat and flavor into the broth. The result is thick, intensely savory and remarkably filling. The beans must be fabes de la Granja or similar large, tender varieties. Served in shallow bowls with crusty bread. One bowl is a complete meal. Mountain comfort food.

2. **Cachopo** — An enormous breaded and fried veal or beef cutlet, pounded thin and folded around a filling of jamón serrano and cheese, then breaded and deep-fried until golden. When cut open, melted cheese and ham flow out. The meat remains tender inside the crispy coating. Served on large platters with fries and peppers. Originally from the mining valleys. Designed to feed hungry workers. So large it often needs to be shared. Variations include mushroom or apple fillings.

3. **Queso de Cabrales** — One of Spain's great blue cheeses, made from cow, sheep and goat milk, aged in natural limestone caves in the Picos de Europa mountains. The humid cave conditions create strong blue veining throughout. The flavor is intense, pungent, salty and sharp, with a creamy, slightly grainy texture. The rind is natural and sticky. Best eaten at room temperature with bread, apple or honey. Wrapped in leaves traditionally. Protected designation of origin. Distinctly powerful cheese not for timid palates.

4. **Sidra** — Hard cider, Asturias's signature drink, made from local apple varieties. Traditionally poured from height (escanciar) into wide, flat glasses, creating foam and releasing aromatics. Only a small amount is poured at a time, drunk in one gulp, then the remainder discarded. The cider is dry, tart, slightly funky and low in alcohol. Drunk at sidrerías (cider houses) where it flows freely. The pouring technique is an art form. Natural, still and cloudy. Completely different from sweet commercial ciders.

5. **Pixín** — Monkfish, prized in Asturias for its firm, meaty texture and sweet flavor. Usually prepared simply—grilled, baked in cider or cooked in a garlic and paprika sauce. The tail meat is the prize, thick and meaty like lobster. The head and bones make excellent stock. Sometimes called the poor man's lobster. Fresh from Cantabrian waters. Often served with potatoes or white beans. The simplicity of preparation showcases the quality of the fish.

6. **Frixuelos** — Very thin crêpes made with flour, eggs, milk and sometimes anise liqueur, cooked until lacy and golden. Eaten plain, rolled with sugar, or filled with cream, chocolate or fruit preserves. The batter should be liquid enough to spread thinly. Traditional for Carnival but eaten year-round. Similar to French crêpes but often flavored with anise. Every family makes them. Children love them plain with sugar. Can be stacked high like pancakes.

7. **Arroz con leche** — Rice pudding, a beloved dessert throughout Spain but especially in Asturias. Short-grain rice is cooked slowly in milk with sugar, cinnamon stick and lemon peel until thick and creamy. Served cold, dusted with cinnamon, sometimes with caramelized sugar on top. The texture should be thick but still flowing, the rice tender but distinct. Every grandmother's recipe is different. Comfort dessert. The cinnamon and lemon give brightness to the rich, milky sweetness.

8. **Tortos** — Thick corn flatbreads cooked on a griddle, similar to cornbread but denser. Made simply from cornmeal, water and salt, formed into rounds and cooked until firm. Eaten hot, often topped with chorizo, fried eggs, or cheese. The corn flavor is pronounced, slightly sweet. Traditional mountain food. The texture is compact and filling. Similar to polenta cakes but thicker. Often accompanies bean dishes or stews. Represents Asturian corn-growing heritage.

9. **Pote asturiano** — A hearty bean, greens and pork stew, similar to Galician caldo but with Asturian variations. White beans, collard greens or cabbage, potatoes, chorizo, and pork shoulder or ribs simmered together. The broth is rich and fatty, the beans creamy, the greens tender. Every valley has variations. Essential winter food. The slow cooking melds all flavors. Served steaming with corn bread. Working-class food that sustained miners and farmers. One-pot complete nutrition.

10. **Casadielles** — Fried pastry turnovers filled with a mixture of ground walnuts, sugar, anise and sometimes honey. The dough is rolled thin, filled, folded into half-moons and deep-fried until golden and crispy. Dusted with sugar while hot. The walnut filling is sweet and aromatic, the pastry shatters delicately. Traditional for Carnival. Found in bakeries but best homemade. The anise gives distinctive flavor. Similar to empanadas but sweet. Best eaten fresh when the pastry is still crispy.

### Drinks
- **Asturian cider (sidra natural)** — Natural, still cider with funky, tart, slightly acidic character. Made from local apple varieties, allowed to ferment naturally without added yeasts. Cloudy, completely dry, low alcohol (5-6%). The traditional pouring (escanciado) from shoulder height into wide glasses held low aerates the cider and creates momentary foam. Only a small amount poured, drunk in one gulp, remainder discarded. Consumed at sidrerías with traditional foods. The ritual is essential. Sharp, refreshing, with barnyard funk. An acquired taste. Cider season January-May when new batches released.
- **Orujo** — Galician-style pomace brandy also produced in Asturias, clear and fiery. Made from grape pomace left from winemaking. Harsh when young, smoother with age. Standard digestif after heavy fabada or other rich foods. Often served in small glass shots called chupitos. Some flavored versions with herbs, honey or cream. The cream orujo (orujo con crema) is sweet and popular as dessert drink. Traditionally moonshine but now legally produced. Helps digest heavy mountain food. Burns pleasantly going down.
- **DOP Cangas wine** — Rare wine denomination from the Cangas del Narcea area of western Asturias. Primarily red wines from local Albarín Negro and Verdejo Negro grapes, unique to the region. The wines are light to medium-bodied with red fruit flavors and good acidity. Very limited production, hard to find outside Asturias. White wines also made from Albarín Blanco. The mountainous terrain makes viticulture challenging. These wines represent Asturian winemaking tradition preserved against odds. Rustic and food-friendly, pair well with local mountain cuisine.
