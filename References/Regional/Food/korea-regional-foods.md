# Korea — Regional Foods

---

## Overview

Korean cuisine in 1913 is structured around rice as the staple grain in the south and millet, sorghum and barley in the north where rice cultivation is less reliable. Every meal centers on a bowl of grain with banchan (side dishes) arranged around it. Fermented foods are foundational: kimchi in dozens of regional varieties, doenjang (fermented soybean paste), ganjang (soy sauce) and gochujang (fermented chili paste, widespread since the 18th century). Meals are communal and shared from central dishes. Soup or jjigae accompanies every proper meal.

---

## Staple Grains and Starches

**Rice** *(bap)* — Short-grain japonica rice grown in southern and central lowlands. Steamed and served as the meal center. Mixed with barley (boribap) in poorer households to stretch supplies. In 1913 white polished rice is a luxury in rural areas; brown or partially milled rice more common outside cities and yangban households. In modern times white rice is universal.

**Barley** *(bori)* — Major grain in poorer households and northern zones. Boribap (barley rice) the everyday staple for farming families in 1913. Barley tea (boricha) served cold in summer and hot in winter instead of plain water, a practice unchanged in modern times.

**Millet** *(jo)* — Northern staple grain where rice cultivation fails. Mixed with rice or eaten alone. Increasingly marginal in modern times.

**Sorghum** *(susu)* — Northern grain. Used in tteok (rice cakes) and porridge. Still present in traditional foods in modern times but not a daily staple.

**Buckwheat** *(memil)* — Highland and northern grain. Made into naengmyeon noodles (cold buckwheat noodles) in Pyongyang style and into jeon (pancakes) in Gangwon province. Naengmyeon is one of the most famous Korean dishes in modern times.

**Sweet Potato** *(goguma)* — Introduced centuries earlier from Japan. Roasted whole in embers in winter, eaten as snack or supplementary staple by poorer households in 1913. Ubiquitous street food in modern Korean winters.

**Taro** *(toranguk)* — Root vegetable used in autumn harvest soups, especially in ritual and ancestral ceremony contexts.

## Fermented Foundations

**Kimchi** — Fermented vegetables, most commonly napa cabbage (baechu kimchi) seasoned with gochugaru (red pepper flakes), garlic, ginger, salted shrimp and fish sauce. Made in large quantities in late autumn (kimjang) and stored in earthenware onggi crocks buried to the neck in the ground for temperature stability. Regional varieties include kkakdugi (cubed radish), oi sobagi (cucumber) and nabak kimchi (water kimchi, mild). In 1913 kimchi is made without artificial refrigeration entirely through fermentation chemistry. In modern times kimchi refrigerators are dedicated appliances in most Korean households.

**Doenjang** — Fermented soybean paste made from meju (dried soybean blocks inoculated with mold). The liquid drawn off during fermentation becomes ganjang (soy sauce). Both are household-made in 1913 in every region. Industrial production dominates in modern times but artisanal doenjang remains valued.

**Gochujang** — Fermented chili and soybean paste with glutinous rice. Thick, sweet, spicy and deeply savory. Made in large batches and aged in onggi crocks on rooftop platforms (jangdokdae). Essential to bibimbap, tteokbokki and dozens of other dishes. Present in 1913 in all regions south of the northern provinces.

**Jeotgal** — Salted fermented seafood used as a seasoning ingredient in kimchi and eaten alone as banchan. Varieties include saeujeot (salted shrimp), ojingeo-jeot (squid) and gejang (raw salted crab). Regional specialties across the entire coast in 1913 and modern times.

## Regional Dishes by Province

### Gyeonggi (Seoul and surrounds)

**Seolleongtang** — Long-simmered ox bone and brisket soup. Milky white broth from collagen. Seasoned at the table with salt and green onion. A Seoul winter staple in 1913 sold from dedicated restaurants. Unchanged in modern times.

**Galbi** — Beef short rib. Marinated in soy sauce, sugar, garlic, pear and sesame oil then grilled over charcoal. In 1913 a luxury dish eaten at special occasions and yangban households. In modern times galbi restaurants are everywhere.

**Tteokguk** — Sliced rice cake soup eaten on New Year's Day. Consuming it is said to add a year to one's age. Beef broth base. Unchanged across 1913 and modern times as a ritual food.

**Bibimbap** — Mixed rice bowl with namul (seasoned vegetables), gochujang, a fried or raw egg and often beef. The Jeonju version is most famous but Seoul versions with royal court influence use more elaborate garnishes. Eaten in 1913 as both everyday and ceremonial food.

### Pyongan (Pyongyang region, northern)

**Naengmyeon** — Cold noodles in chilled beef or pheasant broth. Buckwheat noodles, thin and chewy. Topped with sliced beef, cucumber, Asian pear, a hard-boiled egg and mustard. Pyongyang naengmyeon is the original form; in 1913 a summer dish and a northern specialty. In modern South Korea it is eaten year-round and sold in dedicated restaurants.

**Pyongyang Onban** — Chicken broth rice with chicken, egg garnish and pine nuts. Delicate northern court-influenced dish.

**Sundae** — Blood sausage made from pig intestine stuffed with glass noodles, vegetables, barley and pork blood. Northern origin. In modern times a street food throughout Korea.

### Hamgyong (Far northeast, mountainous)

**Hamhung Naengmyeon** — Spicy starch noodles (potato or arrowroot) rather than buckwheat. Dressed in gochujang sauce rather than broth. Chewier and spicier than Pyongyang style. A distinct regional variant in 1913.

**Gajami Sikhae** — Fermented flatfish and grain dish unique to the Hamgyong coast. Grain ferments with the fish over weeks, producing a sour and intensely flavored preserved food.

### Jeolla (Southwest, richest agricultural region)

**Jeonju Bibimbap** — The most elaborate bibimbap. Over thirty types of namul, beef tartare, bean sprout and sesame. Served in a stone bowl or with a garnish of chrysanthemum and pine nut. In 1913 Jeonju is the acknowledged capital of Korean cuisine. In modern times Jeonju bibimbap is internationally recognized.

**Kimchi Jjigae** — Fermented kimchi stewed with pork, tofu and gochujang. The definitive Korean stew in 1913 and modern times. Jeolla versions are particularly rich.

**Ganjang Gejang** — Raw blue crab marinated in soy sauce. Called "rice thief" because it is so flavorful it drives excessive rice consumption. A Jeolla and South Chungcheong specialty. Unchanged in modern times.

**Hongeo** — Fermented skate. Ammoniated through natural fermentation of the cartilaginous fish. Extremely pungent. A Heuksando island specialty eaten at ceremonial meals with pork and kimchi (hongeo samhap). Considered an acquired taste; unchanged in modern times.

### Gyeongsang (Southeast)

**Dwaeji Gukbap** — Pork and rice soup. Busan specialty. Pork broth with rice, intestines, blood sausage and vegetables. Eaten for breakfast in 1913 and modern times at Busan markets.

**Milmyeon** — Wheat flour noodles in cold beef broth. A Busan variation on cold noodles developed partly from northern refugee influence after later conflicts. Already present as a wheat noodle tradition in 1913.

**Daegu Tang** — Codfish soup. Named for the city Daegu but also a pun on the Korean word for cod (daegutang). Clear or mildly spicy broth with cod, radish and green onion.

### Jeju Island

**Heukdwaeji** — Black pork from the native Jeju black pig breed. Grilled and eaten with raw garlic, salt and ssamjang wrapped in lettuce. The defining Jeju meat dish in 1913 and modern times.

**Jeonbokjuk** — Abalone porridge. Rice slow-cooked with chopped abalone and sesame oil. Considered a restorative and luxury food. Jeju haenyeo dive for abalone in 1913 as they do in modern times.

**Okdom Gui** — Grilled red tilefish. The prestige fish of Jeju, salted and air-dried before grilling. Presented at formal meals and ancestral rites.

## Street and Everyday Foods

**Tteok** — Rice cakes in hundreds of regional forms. Injeolmi (pounded glutinous rice coated in roasted soybean powder), songpyeon (half-moon shaped, pine needle steamed, filled with sesame or chestnut), garaetteok (cylinder shaped, sliced for tteokguk). Made for every major festival and ceremony. In 1913 made at home or purchased from tteok shops. In modern times tteok bakeries operate alongside convenience store packaged versions.

**Jeon** — Savory pancakes. Pajeon (green onion), haemul pajeon (seafood and green onion), kimchijeon (kimchi). Cooked on iron griddles at home and at market stalls. Eaten at festivals and rainy days (a cultural association persisting into modern times).

**Japchae** — Glass noodles stir-fried with vegetables and beef in sesame oil and soy sauce. Court dish origin. In 1913 a festive and celebratory food. In modern times served at nearly every Korean celebration and increasingly everyday.

**Bossam** — Boiled pork belly eaten wrapped in salted napa cabbage with oyster, kimchi and fermented shrimp paste. A winter dish tied to kimjang season in 1913. A beloved casual restaurant dish in modern times.

## Drinks

**Makgeolli** — Unfiltered rice wine. Milky white, slightly fizzy, low alcohol. Farmer's drink in 1913, sold from earthenware jugs at market stalls. Drunk with pajeon in rain. In modern times experiencing a craft revival and exported internationally.

**Soju** — Distilled spirit traditionally from grain (not the sweet potato and ethanol blend that dominates the modern mass market). In 1913 a clear grain distillate drunk neat at meals and celebrations. In modern times the world's best-selling spirit by volume in its mass-market form.

**Sikhye** — Sweet rice punch. Made from malted barley water poured over cooked rice and left to ferment slightly, producing a sweet non-alcoholic drink with floating rice grains. Served cold at the end of meals and at festivals. Unchanged in modern times.

**Omija Cha** — Five-flavor berry tea. Dried schisandra berries steeped cold. Sweet, sour, salty, bitter and pungent simultaneously. Served cold in summer. Present in 1913 and modern times in traditional tea houses.

**Boricha** — Roasted barley tea. Served instead of plain water at meals and in households. In 1913 universal in non-noble households as an economical daily drink. In modern times served free at Korean restaurants and bottled commercially.

## Dining Structure

In 1913 Korean meals are served with all dishes simultaneously on a low table (soban), diners seated on floor cushions (bang-seok). Chopsticks and a long-handled spoon are the utensils. The spoon is for soup and rice; chopsticks for everything else. It is considered impolite to lift the rice bowl from the table (unlike Japanese and Chinese practice). Meals follow a strict hierarchy of seating by age and rank. The eldest eats first or at minimum begins before others.

Restaurants (sikdang) operate in towns and cities in 1913, often specializing in a single dish. Market meals are eaten standing or on low benches. Taverns (jujeom) serve makgeolli with anju (drinking food, usually jeon or dried fish).

In modern times floor seating persists in traditional restaurants alongside Western table seating. Communal eating culture is unchanged. Korean barbecue (samgyeopsal, galbi) at table grills is a modern development from the mid-20th century.
