# China — Regional Foods

---

## Overview

Chinese cuisine in 1913 is already the most regionally diverse food culture on earth. The Eight Great Cuisines framework (Sichuan, Cantonese, Jiangsu, Shandong, Fujian, Hunan, Anhui, Zhejiang) exists as a recognized concept among educated Chinese though regional identity is the stronger organizing principle at the village and city level. Climate, terrain, available ingredients and historical trade routes determine each region's character. The north eats wheat; the south eats rice. The interior eats preserved and fermented; the coast eats fresh. The southwest eats fiery and numbing; the east eats sweet and delicate.

---

## Staple Grains and Starches

**Rice** *(Oryza sativa)* — The dominant grain south of the Huai River. Short-grain japonica in the northeast; long-grain indica in the south. Steamed, congeed (jook/zhou), fermented into wine, ground into flour for noodles and cakes. In 1913 the most consumed grain by volume in China. In modern times China is the world's largest rice producer and consumer.

**Wheat** *(Triticum aestivum)* — Dominant grain north of the Huai River. Made into noodles (la mian, dao xiao mian, zha jiang mian), steamed buns (mantou, baozi), dumplings (jiaozi, wonton), pancakes (bing) and flatbreads. Northern Chinese wheat culture is as sophisticated and regionally varied as rice culture in the south. Unchanged in modern times.

**Millet** *(Setaria italica)* — Ancient staple of the northern loess plateau and drought-prone regions. Cooked as porridge or steamed grain. Still eaten in Shanxi, Shaanxi and Gansu in 1913 as a daily staple. In modern times a specialty grain associated with traditional diets and health food markets.

**Taro** *(Colocasia esculenta)* — Root vegetable in southern and coastal regions. Steamed, braised or made into taro cake (wu tou gao) in Cantonese cuisine. Present in 1913 and modern times.

**Sweet Potato** *(Ipomoea batatas)* — Major supplementary staple across central and southern China, especially among poorer households in 1913. Roasted whole and sold from street carts in winter. Dried into strips for storage. In modern times still a comfort street food.

## Northern Cuisine (Shandong / Beijing / Hebei)

**Shandong (Lu Cuisine)** — Considered the oldest and most foundational of the eight great cuisines. Emphasizes fresh ingredients cooked simply. The dominant influence on Beijing imperial court cuisine. Seafood from the Shandong coast, freshwater fish from rivers and lakes, pork and lamb from inland areas.

**Beijing Roast Duck (Peking Duck)** — Whole duck roasted in a sealed or hanging oven until skin crisps. Carved tableside and eaten in thin pancakes with scallion, cucumber and sweet bean sauce (tianmian jiang). In 1913 associated with the capital's specialized roast duck restaurants (most famous: Quanjude, operating since 1864). The definitive Beijing prestige dish. Unchanged in modern times; internationally recognized.

**Jiaozi** — Filled dumplings with thin wheat dough wrapper and pork, cabbage, chive or other fillings. Boiled (shuijiao), steamed (zhengijiao) or pan-fried (guotie). The quintessential northern Chinese food. Eaten at New Year and all celebrations. In 1913 made at home communally. In modern times available everywhere.

**Zha Jiang Mian** — Thick wheat noodles topped with fermented soybean paste and pork sauce, fresh cucumber, bean sprouts and radish. A Beijing working-class everyday dish in 1913. Still the classic Beijing noodle in modern times.

**Lamb Hot Pot (Shuan Yangrou)** — Thin-sliced lamb cooked by the diner in a charcoal-heated copper pot of boiling broth, dipped in sesame paste sauce. A Mongolian-influenced winter dish in Beijing. In 1913 eaten at dedicated hot pot restaurants in the cold season. In modern times Beijing-style lamb hot pot coexists with the Sichuan spicy variant.

**Mantou** — Plain steamed wheat buns. No filling. The bread of the north, eaten with vegetables, meat dishes or fermented bean paste. In 1913 the everyday carbohydrate of northern Chinese households. In modern times still a staple alongside commercial bread.

## Sichuan Cuisine

**Character:** The boldest regional cuisine in China. Defined by mala (numbing-spicy) flavor from the combination of Sichuan peppercorn (huajiao) and dried chili. The Sichuan peppercorn produces a distinctive tingling numbness on the tongue and lips unlike any other spice. Preserved vegetables, fermented bean paste (doubanjiang from Pixian) and complex layered sauces are foundational.

**Mapo Tofu** — Silken tofu cubed and braised in sauce of ground pork, doubanjiang, fermented black beans, chili oil and Sichuan peppercorn. Named for a pockmarked (ma) old woman (po) in Chengdu folklore. In 1913 a Chengdu restaurant specialty. In modern times one of the most internationally recognized Chinese dishes.

**Kung Pao Chicken (Gong Bao Ji Ding)** — Diced chicken wok-fried with dried chilies, peanuts, Sichuan peppercorn and scallion in a sweet-sour-spicy sauce. Named for a late Qing official. In 1913 established in Sichuan restaurants. In modern times served worldwide; the Americanized version differs substantially from the Sichuan original.

**Dan Dan Noodles** — Wheat noodles in a sauce of sesame paste, chili oil, Sichuan peppercorn, preserved vegetables (ya cai) and ground pork. Originally a street food sold by vendors carrying the dish on a pole (dan dan). In 1913 Chengdu street food. In modern times a restaurant standard.

**Hotpot (Huoguo)** — Communal cooking in a boiling broth. Chongqing and Chengdu style: deep red spicy broth thick with chili and Sichuan peppercorn. Raw ingredients (tripe, brain, duck intestine, beef, vegetables, tofu) cooked by the diner. In 1913 a Chongqing specialty. In modern times the most popular restaurant format in China by revenue.

**Twice-Cooked Pork (Hui Guo Rou)** — Pork belly first boiled then sliced and stir-fried with leek, cabbage and doubanjiang until edges curl and fat renders. A Chengdu home standard in 1913 and modern times.

## Cantonese Cuisine (Guangdong)

**Character:** The most internationally recognized Chinese cuisine due to Cantonese emigration to Southeast Asia, the Americas and Europe. Values freshness of ingredient above all. Minimal heavy spicing to let natural flavors speak. Steaming, quick stir-frying and roasting are primary techniques. Sweet and savory balance is important. Cantonese cooks will eat almost any ingredient if it is fresh.

**Dim Sum (Yum Cha)** — The Cantonese tradition of drinking tea with small dishes. In 1913 practiced at teahouses (chalou) in Guangzhou and Hong Kong. Har gow (shrimp dumpling with translucent wrapper), siu mai (pork and shrimp dumpling), char siu bao (barbecue pork bun), cheung fun (rice noodle roll), dan tat (egg tart), lo mai gai (sticky rice in lotus leaf). In modern times yum cha is a weekend family institution in Cantonese communities globally.

**Char Siu (Cantonese Barbecue Pork)** — Pork shoulder or belly marinated in honey, fermented bean curd, five-spice, soy sauce and roasted hanging in a charcoal oven until caramelized. Sliced over rice or in buns. In 1913 a Guangzhou roast shop specialty. In modern times a global Chinese restaurant standard.

**Cantonese Roast Duck** — Whole duck roasted to mahogany-glazed skin. Different from Peking duck: the Cantonese version uses a different marinade and the skin is not the sole focus. Chopped and served over rice with sweet soy sauce. In 1913 and modern times available at roast meat shops (siu mei shops) across Guangdong.

**Steamed Fish** — Fresh whole fish steamed briefly and finished with hot oil poured over ginger, scallion and soy sauce. The quality of the fish is non-negotiable; the technique is minimal by design. In 1913 the Cantonese live seafood tank restaurant is already an institution in coastal cities. In modern times the standard of freshness in Cantonese cooking is internationally noted.

**Congee (Jook/Zhou)* — Rice slow-cooked in excess water until broken down into silky porridge. Topped with century egg and pork, fish, or dried seafood. The universal Cantonese comfort food in 1913 and modern times. Eaten for breakfast, for illness, at late night restaurants.

## Jiangsu and Zhejiang Cuisine (Huaiyang / Shanghainese)

**Character:** The refined cuisine of the prosperous Yangtze delta. Sweetness is characteristic; sugar and Shaoxing wine appear in savory dishes. Braised meats cooked low and slow until fat becomes gelatinous. Fresh river fish and lake crab are prestige ingredients. Knife work is emphasized; presentation is careful.

**Dongpo Pork** — Pork belly braised for hours in soy sauce, Shaoxing wine and sugar until fat liquefies and meat falls apart. Named for the Song dynasty poet Su Dongpo. In 1913 a Hangzhou specialty. In modern times served at Hangzhou restaurants as a regional emblem.

**Hairy Crab (Dazhaxie)** — Mitten crab from Lake Tai and Yangcheng Lake, harvested in autumn when the roe is full. Steamed simply and eaten with ginger vinegar dipping sauce. In 1913 the most expensive seasonal delicacy of the Jiangnan region, available for only a few weeks a year. In modern times prices for genuine Yangcheng Lake hairy crab reach hundreds of dollars per crab; the season is a major food event.

**Lion's Head Meatballs (Shi Zi Tou)* — Large pork meatballs braised with cabbage until tender. The meatball represents the lion's head; the cabbage the mane. A Yangzhou specialty in 1913. In modern times a Jiangsu restaurant standard.

**Red-Braised Pork (Hong Shao Rou)** — A Jiangsu and Shanghai method for pork belly: soy sauce, sugar, Shaoxing wine and aromatics braised until deep red-brown and intensely flavored. Every Chinese region has a variant. The Jiangnan version is sweeter; the Hunan version (associated with Mao Zedong who reportedly loved it) is spicier.

**Xiaolongbao** — Soup dumplings. Thin-skinned steamed dumplings containing seasoned pork and aspic that liquefies into hot soup when cooked. A Shanghai and Wuxi specialty. In 1913 a teahouse and dumpling shop food. In modern times internationally famous; Din Tai Fung (Taiwan origin) made this style globally recognized.

## Hunan Cuisine

**Character:** Similar in chili use to Sichuan but without the numbing peppercorn. Pure heat. Also characterized by heavy use of preserved and smoked ingredients: cured pork (la rou), preserved vegetables (mei cai), smoked tofu. Hunan food is sharper and more straightforward in its heat than Sichuan's complex layering.

**Steamed Fish Head with Chopped Chili** — Whole fish head steamed under a blanket of fresh chopped red and green chili, garlic and soy sauce. A Hunan specialty in 1913 and modern times. Intensely spicy.

**Mao's Braised Pork** — Red-braised pork belly associated with Mao Zedong's birthplace of Shaoshan. Less sweet than Jiangnan versions; darker, spicier. In modern times a dish of political and culinary pilgrimage in Hunan.

## Street Foods (Nationwide Variants)

**Bing** — Flatbreads in endless regional variation. Sesame-crusted shaobing in the north; scallion oil pancakes (congyoubing) in Shanghai; folded and griddle-cooked jianbing (egg and scallion crêpe with fermented bean paste and chili, a northern breakfast) in 1913 and modern times.

**Tofu** — Fresh, silken, firm, dried, fermented (stinky tofu, chou doufu) in regional variation. Sold from street carts. Stinky tofu deep-fried on street grills is a night market standard in 1913 and modern times.

**Wontons (Huntun)** — Thin-skinned pork-filled dumplings in broth. A southern noodle shop staple. In 1913 sold at market stalls. In modern times available at breakfast shops and noodle restaurants throughout the south.

## Drinks

**Tea** — The universal drink. Drunk throughout the day, before and after meals, in social settings. Green tea in the south and east; compressed pu-erh in Yunnan and Tibet; jasmine-scented tea in Beijing; oolong in Fujian and Guangdong. In 1913 tea is drunk from lidded cups (gaiwan) or small teapots. Teahouses are social centers in every city. In modern times bubble tea, canned tea and tea chains (Heytea, Nayuki) are modern developments layered on the same fundamental culture.

**Baijiu** — Clear grain spirit distilled from sorghum (and sometimes wheat or rice). Intense: 40 to 60 percent alcohol. Drunk at banquets, business meals and celebrations. Maotai (from Guizhou, made from sorghum) is the highest-prestige variant in 1913 and modern times. Shaoxing rice wine (huangjiu), lower alcohol and used in cooking and drinking, is distinct and older.

**Soy Milk (Doujiang)** — Ground soybean liquid drunk hot with youtiao (fried dough sticks) as the classic northern breakfast pair. In 1913 sold by street vendors at dawn. In modern times sold at breakfast chains and convenience stores.

## Dining Structure

In 1913 Chinese meals are served family-style: all dishes placed on the table simultaneously and shared communally. Rice is served in individual bowls. The host places food for honored guests. Chopsticks and a ceramic spoon for soup. Drinking rice wine or tea at meals is common. Banquet culture follows strict protocols of toasting (ganbei) and course order.

Restaurant culture in 1913 is highly developed in Chinese cities. Dedicated restaurants for regional specialties operate in Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Chengdu and Wuhan. Tea houses serve morning dim sum. Night markets operate in southern cities. Food vendor stalls at city gates and markets serve working people.

In modern times table service, kitchen technology and international influence have transformed Chinese restaurants globally while banquet culture, family-style sharing and tea culture remain unchanged in fundamental structure.
