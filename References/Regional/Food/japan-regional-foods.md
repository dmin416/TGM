# Japan — Regional Foods

---

## Overview

Japanese cuisine in 1913 is structured around the tripartite combination of rice, miso soup and pickles (tsukemono) at every meal, with fish, vegetables and tofu as primary protein and accompaniment sources. Beef consumption is a very recent development (legally permitted only from 1872 following the Meiji opening); in 1913 it is available in cities but not yet a cultural foundation. The eight great regional traditions of Japanese cuisine track the same geographic and climatic divisions as the broader cultural regions. The dominant flavor vocabulary uses soy sauce (shoyu), mirin, sake, dashi (fish stock from kombu and katsuobushi) and miso in regional variation of salt, sweetness and depth.

---

## Staple Grains and Starches

**Rice (Gohan)** *(Oryza sativa japonica)* — Short-grain sticky Japanese rice. The absolute center of every meal. Steamed into a slightly sticky mass, served in individual bowls. The quality of rice is the measure of the meal; in 1913 white polished rice is a luxury that poorer households supplement with barley (mugigohan). Niigata (Koshihikari) and Akita (Akitakomachi) varieties are premium in modern times. In 1913 regional rice varieties have not yet been scientifically standardized but Niigata and Akita reputations for quality already exist.

**Mochi (Glutinous Rice Cake)** — Short-grain glutinous rice pounded in a wooden mortar (usu) until it becomes a smooth elastic mass. Shaped into round cakes or squares. Eaten during New Year, grilled (yaki mochi) or in soup (ozoni, New Year's soup). The pounding of mochi at New Year is a communal ritual unchanged in character from 1913 to modern times (though mechanical mochi makers are now common).

**Soba (Buckwheat Noodles)** — Thin grey-brown noodles made from buckwheat flour, sometimes blended with wheat. Eaten cold with dipping sauce (zaru soba) or hot in broth. A Tokyo and Nagano specialty. Soba on New Year's Eve (toshikoshi soba) for longevity is a national tradition in 1913 and modern times.

**Udon (Wheat Noodles)** — Thick white wheat flour noodles. Eaten in hot broth or cold with dipping sauce. Sanuki (Kagawa Prefecture on Shikoku) udon is the most famous regional style: firm, chewy, served simply. In 1913 udon is the dominant noodle form in western Japan as soba is in the east. In modern times Kagawa's udon tourism (udon pilgrimages) is a recognized phenomenon.

## Regional Cuisine by Area

### Kanto (Tokyo / Edo)

**Character:** Strong, dark, salty flavors. Kanto uses darker tamari-style soy sauce (koikuchi) and heavy dashi seasoning. The legacy of the warrior class (samurai) who preferred bold, unambiguous flavors over the delicate subtlety of Kyoto court cuisine.

**Edo-Mae Sushi** — The original Tokyo-style sushi. In 1913 this is nigiri sushi: vinegared rice shaped by hand and topped with fresh raw or cooked seafood from Tokyo Bay. Edo-mae refers to "in front of Edo (Tokyo)." The seafood is Tokyo Bay fish: tuna (maguro), flounder (hirame), horse mackerel (aji), conger eel (anago), clam (hamaguri). In 1913 sushi is still largely street food, eaten standing at outdoor stalls. The fish must be eaten the same day. Refrigeration transforms the industry in the 20th century. In modern times Tokyo sushi is globally exported and high-end omakase restaurants charge thousands of dollars.

**Monjayaki** — A looser, wetter version of savory pancake (okonomiyaki) specific to Tokyo's shitamachi (old downtown) neighborhoods. Batter poured into a ring of fillings on a teppan griddle, mixed and scraped as it cooks into a molten, slightly crispy mixture. A working-class Kanto street food in 1913.

**Shoyu Ramen (Tokyo Style)** — Clear chicken and dashi broth seasoned with soy sauce. Thin wavy noodles. Toppings of chashu pork, nbamboo shoots (menma), nori and green onion. In 1913 ramen (then called Shina soba, "Chinese noodles") is already spreading through Tokyo as a Chinese-influenced noodle form. In modern times Tokyo ramen is a distinct recognized style.

**Sukiyaki** — Thinly sliced beef and vegetables simmered in a sweet soy and mirin broth (warishita) in a cast iron pan at the table, dipped in raw beaten egg before eating. Beef entered Japanese cuisine in the Meiji era and sukiyaki became the primary celebratory beef dish. In 1913 a restaurant and special occasion food for middle-class city households. In modern times a family celebration dish nationwide.

### Kansai / Kinai (Kyoto, Osaka, Nara)

**Character:** Lighter, sweeter flavors than Kanto. Uses light-colored usukuchi soy sauce. Dashi is more prominent. Kyoto cuisine (Kyo-ryori) is refined, vegetable-forward, visually precise; it developed from the Buddhist temple cooking tradition (shojin ryori) and the imperial court.

**Kyoto Kaiseki** — The highest form of Japanese formal cuisine. A multi-course meal with precise seasonal ingredients, elaborate presentation, specific vessel types and a progression from light to rich. In 1913 kaiseki is served at high-end Kyoto restaurants (ryotei) and inns (ryokan). Each course reflects the season in ingredient and visual presentation. Unchanged in modern times as the apex of Japanese formal dining.

**Kyoto Pickles (Kyozuke)** — Kyoto's distinctive preserved vegetables. Shibazuke (purple eggplant and cucumber pickled in shiso and salt), Nishiki pickles (bright yellow and red from turmeric and red pepper), Suguki (turnip fermented in a distinctive sour style unique to Kyoto). In 1913 Kyoto pickle shops operate in Nishiki Market, the city's central food market. In modern times Nishiki Market remains an active food street and Kyoto pickles are a major souvenir.

**Osaka Okonomiyaki** — Savory pancake of shredded cabbage, wheat batter, egg and various mix-ins (pork belly, seafood, cheese) cooked on a teppan griddle. Finished with Worcestershire-style sauce, Japanese mayonnaise, katsuobushi flakes and aonori (dried seaweed). In 1913 Osaka street food. In modern times the definitive Osaka dish, with do-it-yourself teppan restaurants throughout the city.

**Takoyaki** — Octopus balls. Batter poured into hemispherical molds on a special griddle, each mold containing a piece of octopus. Turned with a pick as they cook into round crispy-outside, creamy-inside balls. Finished with the same condiments as okonomiyaki. In 1913 the takoyaki form is just developing as an Osaka street food. In modern times it is inseparable from Osaka identity and found across Japan.

**Fugu (Osaka and Western Japan)** — The consumption of potentially lethal pufferfish is centered in western Japan, particularly Shimonoseki (the primary landing port) and Osaka. In 1913 fugu is eaten at specialist restaurants by those who can afford and trust the preparer. In modern times fugu restaurants throughout western Japan serve licensed preparations.

### Chubu / Nagoya

**Character:** Hatchomiso (dark, long-aged soybean paste) defines Nagoya cuisine. Stronger, earthier and sweeter than other regional miso styles. The red miso base appears in most signature Nagoya dishes.

**Miso Katsu** — Tonkatsu (breaded deep-fried pork cutlet) served with a thick sweet-savory hatchomiso sauce rather than the standard Worcestershire-style sauce. The definitive Nagoya dish in modern times. In 1913 tonkatsu itself is a very recent Meiji-era import from Western-style cooking; the miso variation is developing in the Nagoya restaurant scene.

**Kishimen** — Flat, wide wheat noodles in a soy-based broth. Softer and broader than udon. A Nagoya specialty. In 1913 and modern times sold at Atsuta Shrine station restaurants.

**Ankake Spaghetti** — Thick pasta in a spicy meat sauce of Worcestershire, tomato and other flavors, topped with various ingredients. A Nagoya original developed from Western pasta introduced in the Meiji era. Present in Nagoya's Western-style restaurants in 1913 and a persistent local food in modern times.

### Tohoku (Northeast Honshu)

**Character:** Cold climate cuisine centered on preservation. Heavy salting and fermentation, robust winter dishes, foraged mountain vegetables (sansai). Rice paddies in river valleys but mountainous terrain means diverse supplementary foods.

**Kiritampo** — Cooked rice pounded and molded around a cedar skewer, grilled over charcoal until lightly browned. Can be eaten as is or cut into pieces and simmered in chicken broth (kiritampo nabe). An Akita specialty in 1913 and modern times.

**Imoni** — Taro root hot pot with meat and vegetables. A Yamagata autumn tradition. Communities gather outdoors in September and October around large iron pots to cook imoni together. In 1913 a seasonal communal event. In modern times the Yamagata imoni festival (using a pot large enough for a crane to stir) is a famous regional event.

**Salted Salmon (Shiozake)** — Salt-cured salmon, the preserved protein of the Tohoku winter. Kept in cold storage through the winter months. Grilled for breakfast alongside rice and miso soup. In 1913 the foundation of winter diet in Tohoku coastal areas. In modern times salted salmon remains a standard Japanese breakfast ingredient.

### Hokkaido

**Character:** Japan's northern frontier, settled primarily in the Meiji period. Ainu indigenous food culture mixed with Japanese settler and Russian-influenced cooking. Dairy agriculture in Hokkaido (absent in the rest of Japan) creates a distinct dairy-using food culture.

**Hokkaido Ramen** — Sapporo-style miso ramen: rich miso-based broth, corn, butter, bean sprouts and chashu pork. The butter-corn combination is specific to Hokkaido's dairy culture. In 1913 ramen is arriving in Hokkaido through the growing noodle culture. The miso-butter-corn style consolidates as a distinct Sapporo identity through the early-to-mid 20th century. In modern times Sapporo miso ramen is one of the three great ramen styles alongside Hakata tonkotsu and Tokyo shoyu.

**Jingisukan (Genghis Khan)** — Lamb and vegetables grilled on a domed iron griddle. Named fancifully for the Mongolian conqueror. Lamb is not traditional Japanese meat but Hokkaido's sheep farming culture (developed in the Meiji era) makes it available. In 1913 lamb is being introduced. In modern times jingisukan is the definitive Hokkaido barbecue.

**Hokkaido Dairy** — Fresh milk, butter, cheese and cream are produced in Hokkaido in quantities unavailable elsewhere in Japan. Soft serve ice cream, milk caramels and fresh dairy products are major Hokkaido specialties in modern times. In 1913 dairy products are arriving in Japanese cities from Hokkaido as modern cold chain develops.

**Crab (Kani)** — King crab (tarabagani), snow crab (zuwaigani) and horsehair crab (kegani) all harvested from Hokkaido waters. In 1913 crab is consumed locally near fishing ports and shipped preserved. In modern times Hokkaido crab is a major food tourism draw and premium restaurant ingredient across Japan.

### Kyushu

**Character:** Japan's southernmost main island. Close to Korea and China; distinct Chinese and Korean influences in food history. Tonkotsu (pork bone) ramen originated here. Sweet soy sauce. Shochu (distilled spirit) rather than sake as primary alcohol.

**Hakata Tonkotsu Ramen** — Cloudy white pork bone broth simmered for hours until collagen turns the soup opaque and rich. Thin straight noodles. Toppings of chashu, kikurage (wood ear mushrooms), green onion and red pickled ginger. The ramen of Fukuoka's Hakata district. In 1913 the tonkotsu style is developing in Fukuoka. In modern times Hakata tonkotsu is one of Japan's most internationally recognized dishes; chains like Ippudo and Ichiran have exported it globally.

**Basashi (Horse Sashimi)** — Raw horse meat sliced thin and eaten cold. A Kumamoto specialty. Horse meat consumption in Japan is regionally specific. In 1913 available in Kumamoto specialty restaurants. In modern times still a regional Kumamoto product.

**Shochu** — Distilled Japanese spirit made from sweet potato (imo jochu, Kagoshima), barley (mugi jochu, Oita) or rice. Lower alcohol than sake (25 percent typical). The primary alcohol of Kyushu in 1913. In modern times shochu has overtaken sake in total consumption volume in Japan.

### Okinawa (Ryukyu)

**Character:** Historically the independent Ryukyu Kingdom with its own culinary traditions, only fully incorporated into Japan in 1879. Okinawan cuisine is closer to southern Chinese and Southeast Asian cooking than to mainland Japanese. Pork is central (every part of the pig is used). Bitter melon (goya) is a defining ingredient. Awamori (distilled rice spirit) is the local drink.

**Champuru** — Stir-fry. Goya champuru (bitter melon, tofu, egg, pork) is the signature dish of Okinawa. The combination of bitter melon with egg and pork tempering the bitterness is a specifically Okinawan solution to an ingredient that mainland Japan has not incorporated into its cuisine. In 1913 champuru is everyday Okinawan home cooking. In modern times goya champuru is the most recognized Okinawan dish nationally.

**Rafute** — Pork belly braised slowly in awamori, sugar and soy sauce until intensely tender and sweet. Similar in concept to Dongpo pork and the Korean red-braised pork; Okinawa's position as a China-Japan intermediary culture produced this convergent dish. In 1913 a ceremonial and festive food. In modern times a standard Okinawan restaurant dish.

## Street and Market Foods

**Yakitori** — Chicken pieces (or every other part: heart, liver, cartilage, skin) skewered and grilled over charcoal with tare (sweet soy glaze) or salt. In 1913 sold from street stalls at festivals and evening markets. In modern times yakitori restaurants and izakaya are universal.

**Tempura** — Seafood and vegetables dipped in a thin cold batter and deep-fried until lightly crispy. Portuguese-influenced (from the Jesuit missionary period). By 1913 tempura is already an old Edo tradition, served at tempura specialty restaurants (tempuraya) and as street food. In modern times tempura appears on every Japanese restaurant menu globally.

**Onigiri** — Rice balls shaped by hand into triangles or ovals, often with a filling (pickled plum, salted salmon, tuna mayonnaise) and wrapped in nori. Portable, holdable food in 1913 as in modern times. In modern times the convenience store onigiri (individually wrapped, shelf-stable with a special moisture barrier between nori and rice) is a technological and cultural institution selling billions of units annually.

## Drinks

**Green Tea (Matcha, Sencha, Bancha)** — The universal Japanese drink. Drunk at all times and occasions. Sencha (loose leaf, steeped) is the everyday tea. Matcha (powdered, whisked) is used in the tea ceremony (chado) and increasingly as a flavoring ingredient. Bancha (coarser, lower grade) is used for everyday drinking and roasted into hojicha. In 1913 tea ceremony (chado) is a major cultural practice across social classes, especially for women and the educated. In modern times matcha has become a global food flavoring phenomenon.

**Sake (Nihonshu)** — Fermented rice wine. Brewed across Japan with regional variation in style, from dry to sweet. Drunk warm in winter and chilled in summer. The central beverage of ceremony and celebration in 1913. In modern times craft sake production is growing; international export is increasing. Japan's sake-producing regions (Nada in Hyogo, Fushimi in Kyoto, Niigata) are like wine appellations.

**Shochu** — See Kyushu section. In modern times shochu is consumed nationwide, drunk on the rocks (mizuwari, with water), as a highball or with juice.

**Ramune** — Carbonated lemon-lime soft drink in a distinctive glass bottle sealed with a glass marble. Introduced in the Meiji era. A summer festival drink in 1913 and modern times. The marble-sealed bottle is unchanged in design for over a century.
