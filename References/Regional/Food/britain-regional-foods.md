# Britain — Regional Foods and Drinks

## Scotland

### Foods
1. **Haggis** — Scotland's national dish combining minced sheep heart, liver and lungs mixed with oatmeal, onions, suet and spices, traditionally encased in a sheep's stomach lining and boiled. The texture is crumbly and rich, with a distinctive savory, peppery flavor. Served with neeps and tatties (turnips and potatoes) and a dram of whisky. Burns Night celebrations center around this dish with ceremonial addressing of the haggis.

2. **Cullen skink** — A thick, creamy soup from the fishing village of Cullen in Moray. Made with smoked haddock, potatoes, onions and milk or cream. The fish is poached in water, flaked, then combined with softened potatoes to create a rich, warming chowder. The smoky fish flavor dominates while the potatoes provide body and sweetness. Often served with crusty bread.

3. **Scotch pie** — A small, round meat pie with straight sides and a flat top, traditionally filled with seasoned minced mutton or lamb. The hot water crust pastry is sturdy enough to hold without a tin, golden and crisp. The filling is peppery and rich with gravy. Sold hot at football matches and bakeries throughout Scotland. The top crust is designed to hold beans, gravy or mashed potatoes.

4. **Cranachan** — A traditional Scottish dessert made by layering whipped cream with toasted oatmeal, fresh raspberries, honey and a generous splash of whisky. The oats provide nutty crunch against the soft cream, while the raspberries add tartness. Originally a harvest celebration dessert, now served year-round. Some versions include crowdie (soft cheese) for extra richness.

5. **Tattie scone** — A soft, thin flatbread made from mashed potatoes, butter and flour, cooked on a griddle until golden with characteristic brown spots. The texture is dense and slightly chewy, with a mild potato flavor. Essential component of a full Scottish breakfast alongside eggs, bacon, sausage and beans. Also eaten with butter or used to make breakfast sandwiches.

6. **Arbroath smokie** — Whole haddock that are salted, tied in pairs and hot-smoked over hardwood fires until the skin turns coppery gold and the flesh becomes creamy and succulent. The smoking process takes only hours, creating a distinctive sweet, smoky flavor quite different from cold-smoked fish. Named after the town of Arbroath, with protected geographical indication status. Eaten hot or cold, often in pâtés or kedgeree.

7. **Black pudding** — A blood sausage made from pork blood, pork fat, oatmeal, onions and spices, encased in natural casings. The Scottish version is crumblier than English varieties due to the higher oatmeal content. Rich, earthy and slightly metallic in flavor with a distinctive grainy texture. Sliced and fried for breakfast or grilled. Stornoway black pudding from the Western Isles has protected status.

8. **Shortbread** — A rich, crumbly butter cookie with just three ingredients: butter, sugar and flour in a precise ratio. The high butter content creates the characteristic sandy, melt-in-mouth texture. Traditionally pressed into decorative wooden molds before baking. Associated with Christmas and Hogmanay but eaten year-round. Walkers of Aberlour is the most famous commercial producer.

9. **Forfar bridie** — A semicircular meat pastry from the town of Forfar, filled with minced beef, onions and sometimes suet. Unlike Cornish pasties, bridies contain no potato or turnip, just meat and onions. The pastry is crimped along the top rather than the side. Traditionally made with shortcrust pastry, creating a tender, flaky case. Eaten hot as a hearty snack or lunch.

10. **Stovies** — A simple, hearty dish of potatoes cooked slowly with onions and leftover meat (often roast beef or corned beef) until the potatoes break down into a thick, comforting mash. The meat juices and fat create rich flavor. Every family has their own recipe, with some versions quite soupy and others almost dry. Traditionally made on Mondays using Sunday roast leftovers. Served with oatcakes and pickled beetroot.

### Drinks
- Scotch whisky (single malt, blended)
- Irn-Bru (orange fizzy drink)
- Scottish ale (heavy, export styles)

## Wales

### Foods
1. **Welsh rarebit** — A sophisticated cheese sauce made with mature cheddar, ale, mustard, Worcestershire sauce and sometimes egg, spread thick over toasted bread and grilled until golden and bubbling. Far superior to simple cheese on toast, the ale and mustard create complex, tangy depth. The sauce should be thick enough to stay put but creamy enough to coat every bite. Traditional pub food, often served with pickled onions or tomatoes.

2. **Cawl** — Wales's national dish, a hearty one-pot stew traditionally made with lamb or mutton, leeks, potatoes, swedes, carrots and parsnips, simmered for hours until the meat falls off the bone. The long cooking creates rich, deeply savory broth. Historically eaten in two courses: broth with bread first, then the meat and vegetables. Every Welsh family has their own recipe, passed down through generations. Best made a day ahead so flavors deepen.

3. **Bara brith** — A moist, dark tea loaf studded with dried fruits that have been soaked overnight in hot tea. The name means speckled bread in Welsh. Lightly spiced with mixed spice or cinnamon, with a close, tender crumb. The tea-soaking plumps the fruit and adds subtle tannin notes. Sliced thick and spread with salted Welsh butter. Regional variations include adding marmalade or whisky to the soaking liquid.

4. **Laverbread** — Not bread at all, but a puree of laver seaweed (similar to Japanese nori) that has been washed, boiled for hours until soft, then minced to a dark green-black paste. Extremely nutritious, rich in iodine and iron. The flavor is intensely oceanic and mineral, slightly metallic. Traditionally mixed with oatmeal, formed into patties and fried in bacon fat for breakfast. Also eaten with cockles, bacon and eggs as a Welsh breakfast specialty.

5. **Welsh cakes** — Small, round griddle cakes made with flour, butter, sugar, eggs, currants and mixed spice, cooked on a flat griddle or bakestone until golden on both sides with a distinctive middle band. Eaten warm, dusted with sugar. The texture is somewhere between a scone and a cookie, slightly dense but tender. Not to be confused with pancakes or scones. Every Welsh bakery and market stall sells them fresh daily.

6. **Glamorgan sausage** — A vegetarian sausage made from breadcrumbs, Caerphilly cheese, leeks, herbs and mustard, bound with egg and rolled in breadcrumbs before frying. Golden and crispy outside, soft and savory inside. The leeks provide sweetness and moisture while the cheese gives rich, tangy flavor. Named after the historic county of Glamorgan. Originally made with Glamorgan cheese, now extinct, so Caerphilly is substituted.

7. **Lamb cawl** — A variation of traditional cawl specifically highlighting Welsh lamb, which is prized for its sweet, delicate flavor from sheep raised on mountain pastures. The meat is simmered with root vegetables and leeks until tender, creating a nourishing, warming broth. Often served with crusty bread and Caerphilly cheese on the side. Winter comfort food that sticks to your ribs. The fatty cuts like neck or shoulder work best.

8. **Cockles** — Small, sweet shellfish harvested from the beaches of South Wales, particularly around Penclawdd in the Gower Peninsula. Sold cooked, either in their shells or picked out. The flavor is delicate and briny, sweeter than mussels. Traditionally eaten plain with a splash of vinegar and black pepper, or in pies. Often paired with laverbread and bacon for an authentic Welsh breakfast.

9. **Welsh lamb** — Lamb from sheep raised on the mountains and hills of Wales, feeding on wild grasses, heather and herbs. This diet creates meat with exceptional flavor, tender texture and less fatty than lowland lamb. Protected status as PGI (Protected Geographical Indication). The meat is sweet and mild, with no strong gamey taste. Best simply roasted with rosemary and served pink. Salt marsh lamb from coastal areas has particularly complex, herbaceous notes.

10. **Caerphilly cheese** — A young, crumbly white cheese with a mild, slightly lemony flavor and moist texture. One of Wales's few traditional cheeses that survived industrialization. The fresh, clean taste makes it perfect for Welsh rarebit or eating with crusty bread. When very young, it is quite creamy, developing a firmer, more crumbly texture as it ages. Made from cow's milk with a thin rind. Less salty than most white cheeses.

### Drinks
- Welsh whisky (Penderyn distillery)
- Welsh beer (Brains, Felinfoel ales)
- Mead (honey wine, traditional)

## England — Cornwall

### Foods
1. **Cornish pasty** — The iconic crescent-shaped pastry with a distinctive crimped edge along one side, filled with roughly diced beef skirt, potato, swede (rutabaga) and onion, seasoned simply with salt and pepper. Protected by PGI status, must be made in Cornwall to bear the name. The crimped edge was designed so miners could hold the pasty with dirty hands and discard the crust. Eaten hot or cold, the pastry should be golden and sturdy enough to hold the filling without breaking.

2. **Stargazy pie** — A dramatic and unusual fish pie from the village of Mousehole, featuring whole pilchards (sardines) baked in pastry with their heads and tails protruding through the crust, appearing to gaze at the stars. The fish are arranged in a star pattern with eggs and potatoes. Made annually on December 23rd to commemorate Tom Bawcock's Eve, celebrating a legendary fisherman who saved the village from starvation. The exposed fish heads allow the oils to run back into the pie while baking.

3. **Cornish clotted cream** — Thick, rich cream made by gently heating full-cream milk until a golden crust forms on top, which is then skimmed off. The texture is somewhere between butter and whipped cream, smooth and silky with a slightly grainy quality. Minimum 55% butterfat content. The gentle heating gives it a distinctive cooked, almost toffee-like flavor. Essential component of a cream tea. The Cornish method insists cream goes on the scone first, then jam (opposite of Devon).

4. **Saffron bun** — A lightly sweetened yeasted bread enriched with butter, eggs and expensive saffron threads, which give it a distinctive golden yellow color and subtle, slightly medicinal floral flavor. The texture is soft and tender like brioche. Studded with currants or raisins. Traditionally baked for special occasions and holidays. The saffron was brought by Phoenician traders centuries ago. Eaten sliced with butter.

5. **Hevva cake** — A sweet cake made with flour, lard, sugar, currants and candied peel, marked on top with a crosshatch pattern representing fishing nets. The name comes from the cry of hevva (Cornish for haul) shouted when shoals of pilchards were spotted. Originally made for fishermen and their families to celebrate good catches. The texture is dense and rich, similar to a thick cookie or shortbread. Best eaten with tea.

6. **Cornish yarg** — A semi-hard cow's milk cheese wrapped in nettle leaves, which form an edible, attractive gray-green coating that helps the cheese mature. The nettles impart a subtle, slightly mushroomy flavor to the outer layer. The cheese itself is creamy near the rind, becoming crumbly toward the center, with a fresh, mild flavor reminiscent of Caerphilly. Created in the 1980s using a recipe found in a book, named backwards for the creator Alan Gray.

7. **Cornish blue cheese** — A creamy, buttery blue cheese made from Cornish milk with a natural rind. The blue veining is less aggressive than Stilton, creating a milder, sweeter flavor. The texture is smooth and spreadable when young, becoming slightly more crumbly with age. The paste is ivory-white with blue-green marbling. Made by the Cornish Cheese Company. Pairs well with Cornish honey or apple chutney.

8. **Mackerel** — Freshly caught Atlantic mackerel is abundant in Cornish waters during summer months. The oily fish is prized for its rich, distinctive flavor and firm texture. Traditionally grilled whole or filleted and pan-fried, served simply with lemon and butter. Mackerel must be eaten very fresh as the flesh deteriorates quickly. Also smoked, pickled in vinegar, or made into pâté. The silvery-blue skin crisps beautifully when cooked.

9. **Fairings** — Crisp ginger biscuits traditionally sold at fairs (hence the name) throughout Cornwall. Made with flour, sugar, butter, treacle, ginger and mixed spices, they are thin and snap-crisp with intense ginger heat. The flavor is warming and deeply spiced, with a slightly bitter molasses note. Modern commercial versions are made by Furniss of Cornwall. Excellent dunked in tea or served with Cornish ice cream.

10. **Cornish fudge** — Rich, creamy fudge made with Cornish clotted cream, butter, sugar and condensed milk, giving it exceptional smoothness and depth of flavor. The clotted cream adds a distinctive cooked, caramelized note. Available in various flavors including vanilla, chocolate, rum and raisin. The texture is soft and melting rather than grainy. Made by traditional fudge makers in tourist towns like Padstow and St Ives. Sold in traditional striped paper bags.

### Drinks
- Cornish ale (St. Austell, Sharp's breweries)
- Cornish cider (traditional scrumpy)
- Cornish mead (honey wine)
