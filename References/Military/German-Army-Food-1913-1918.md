## Peacetime 1913: Garrison Diet

Soldiers in garrison prepared food cooperatively in groups called **Menagen**, collective cooking communities that purchased food and hired cooks or took turns. The peacetime diet mirrored the German working class civilian diet, shaped by regional custom and rank.

### Daily Peacetime Garrison Meals

**Morning:** Ersatz coffee (roasted grain, not real coffee), 200–250g Kommissbrot with lard or margarine, occasionally cheese

**Midday (main meal):** Potato stew or thick legume soup, small portion of organ meat (heart, liver, kidneys, lungs, rarely prime cuts), rye bread, seasonal vegetables when available

**Evening:** Cold bread, cold cuts, cheese, more ersatz coffee or beer

Lunch and dinner staples: potatoes in many preparations, Kommissbrot, all sorts of cheeses, milk, rice, beans, peas, oats, barley, dried vegetables, dried and preserved fruits. Fresh vegetables were issued in quantity when available, and all cooking water was reused for soups and sauces to preserve vitamins and minerals. Meat was scarce but what existed was usually organ meat, dense in nutrition, cheap, abundant.

**Peacetime caloric total: ~3,200 kcal/day**

## Kommissbrot: The Bread

The caloric backbone of the entire German military diet. A dense, gray sourdough rye bread baked in large loaves using a sourdough fermentation process at 220–260°C to produce a hard crust and extended shelf life.

**1914 recipe composition:** Rye flour, whole wheat flour, cocoa (genuine, adds calories and moisture retention), caraway seeds, salt, sugar, butter, water. Approximately 59% hydration, stiff, coarse, brick dense.

**Nutrition:** ~250–280 kcal/100g. At 750g daily ration: **~1,875–2,100 kcal**, the single largest calorie source in the entire German military diet.

**Wartime adulteration timeline:**

| Year | Potato flour % | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1914 | 5% | Near original recipe |
| 1915 | 20% | Grain shortages biting |
| 1916 | 30%+ | Bran added, texture degrading |
| 1917–18 | Unknown fillers | Sawdust documented in some bakeries |

Soldiers at the front received it stale. Field bakeries were located well behind the lines, and bread arrived cold and hard by the time it reached forward positions. Standard practice was toasting over a candle flame or small trench stove to make it palatable and combat moisture damage.

## The Delivery System: Gulaschkanone

The **Gulaschkanone** (goulash cannon), so named because its chimney resembled a cannon barrel when towed horizontally behind horses. Invented in 1892 by Karl Rudolf Fissler. The defining piece of German military logistics infrastructure.

**Specs:**

- 200 liter main cauldron
- 90 liter coffee boiler
- Ingredient storage in front compartment
- Towed by two horses
- Could cook while moving
- Fed 100–200 men per load

**Standard menu from the Gulaschkanone:**

- Potato soup (Kartoffelsuppe)
- Pea and ham soup (Erbsensuppe mit Speck)
- Various stews: beans, potatoes, whatever meat was available
- Frontkameradensuppe: beans, potatoes, ham ("the comrades")
- Ersatz coffee

**Meal structure when available:**

- Breakfast: 1/6 of daily calories
- Lunch: 1/2 of daily calories
- Evening: 1/3 of daily calories

The objective was one hot meal per soldier per day. Whether that was achieved depended entirely on the tactical situation.

## Erbswurst: The Iron Ration Soup

Invented 1867 by Heinrich Grüneberg. Purchased immediately by the Prussian Army. A compressed pellet of pea flour (~60%), rendered pork fat/bacon (~35%), onions, salt, and spices.

- **One pellet** dissolved in ¼ liter boiling water = one soup ration
- **One roll** = 6 pellets
- **Shelf life:** Near indefinite
- **Calories per pellet:** ~75–100 kcal
- **Calories per full 2L Kochgeschirr:** ~550–600 kcal

Originally designated as Eisernportion, iron ration, not to be consumed except in emergency and on officer's orders. Withdrawn from standard issue early in WWI due to soldier complaints about taste and digestibility, but continued to be sold and issued as emergency supplement.

## What Actually Reached the Soldier: Three Tiers

### Tier 1: Behind the Lines / Rest (Best Case)

| Meal | Food | Approx kcal |
|---|---|---|
| Breakfast | Ersatz coffee, 200–250g Kommissbrot, margarine or lard | ~600 |
| Lunch | Potato and meat stew or pea soup from Gulaschkanone, bread | ~1,500 |
| Evening | Cold bread, cold cuts, cheese, coffee | ~800 |
| **Total** | | **~2,900–3,500** |

### Tier 2: Active Advance / Marching (August–September 1914 Reality)

Supply wagons could not keep pace with the Schlieffen advance. Soldiers foraged from Belgian and French farms, with and without permission.

| Food | Source | kcal |
|---|---|---|
| Kommissbrot (~375g, half ration) | Carried on person | ~975 |
| Erbswurst soup, 1–2 pellets | Iron ration | ~175 |
| Foraged potatoes, apples, vegetables | Local farms | ~400 |
| Gulaschkanone (when it caught up) | Every 2–3 days average | ~600/day avg |
| **Total** | | **~2,000–2,300** |

Significantly below the 4,000+ kcal needed for hard marching. Soldiers were running a caloric deficit from the first weeks of the war.

### Tier 3: Front Line Trench (Late 1914 Onward)

Food carried up communication trenches in dixies, petrol cans, and old jam jars packed in straw lined boxes. Hot food arrived cold. Bread arrived stale. Water tasted of petrol. Hot meals were a rumor on active front days.

What actually arrived:

- Cold, stale Kommissbrot (toasted if possible)
- Cold stew if lucky
- Hardtack biscuit (Hartkeks) as bread substitute
- Erbswurst dissolved in whatever water was available
- Ersatz coffee: roasted grain, chicory, no actual coffee beans
- Tinned Fleischkonserve (canned meat, contents ambiguously labeled)
- Schmalzfleisch (canned pork fat product) on good days

## The Rank Divide

Officers ate from separate messes with access to civilian supplement purchases. A Leutnant in 1914 ate recognizably better in every metric than the Gefreiter beside him. Regional origin also mattered. Bavarian supply chains preferentially supplied Bavarian troops, which generated real animosity. Bavarian soldiers on the Western Front openly declared Prussian infantrymen a greater enemy than the French, partly over food distribution.

## The Blockade Arc: Caloric Collapse

The British naval blockade strangled German food imports progressively from 1914 onward. Its effect on the home front and military ration alike was catastrophic by 1917.

| Period | Actual kcal/day | Bread quality | Hot meals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peacetime 1913 | ~3,200 | Full Kommissbrot | Daily, garrison |
| Aug–Sep 1914 advance | ~2,000–2,300 | Intermittent, stale | Every 2–3 days |
| Late 1914, trenches set | ~2,500–3,000 | Stale, cold | Once daily if lucky |
| 1916–17, blockade peak | ~1,800–2,200 | Adulterated, potato heavy | Irregular |
| 1918 collapse | ~1,500 or less | Essentially inedible | Frequently none |

By 1917, the food allocation aimed only to cover approximately **half the daily calories** needed by an adult. The British blockade did more damage to German fighting capacity than any single battle on the Western Front.

## The Tobacco and Alcohol Ration

Often overlooked but genuinely important for morale and caloric supplementation:

- Two cigars and two cigarettes daily (or pipe tobacco equivalent)
- At commanding officer discretion: spirits, wine (~0.44 pint), or beer (~0.88 pint)
- Beer provided real calories (~40–50 kcal/100ml) and was issued regularly in rear areas

## Summary: The Real German Military Diet

The German army in 1913–1914 was among the best fed armies in the world by contemporary standards. Scientifically designed, high in carbohydrates, adequate in protein, and comprehensive in micronutrients when the full ration was received. The system was sound.

The war broke the system. Not through poor design but through blockade, attrition, and the sheer scale of feeding millions of men in static industrial warfare. By 1918, German soldiers were fighting on rations that would today constitute malnutrition. The collapse of the food supply was a direct contributor to the 1918 mutinies, the November Revolution, and the end of the war.

The Kommissbrot outlasted the Kaiser. It is still produced in Germany today.
