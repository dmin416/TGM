# German Firearms and Ammunition, 1913

OTL reference for *Trenches, Guns and Magic*. Every firearm and cartridge a German soldier could issue, purchase, or use in Germany in 1913: military kit, commercial sporting ammunition, pistol and rimfire rounds, and imported foreign calibers. Germany in 1913 had no meaningful restrictions on civilian firearms or ammunition purchases, and a highly developed commercial arms and ammunition industry (chiefly DWM, RWS/Utendörfer, and smaller Suhl-area makers) that exported and sold a vast range of calibers.

## Firearms

Thorough breakdown of every firearm available to a German soldier in 1913, organized by category.

### Rifles

**Gewehr 98 (Gew 98):** The backbone of the Imperial German Army. Adopted in 1898 as the standard infantry rifle, the Gewehr 98 was the latest in the long line of Mauser rifles and the first to use the M98 controlled-feed bolt-action. It fired the 7.92×57mm S Patrone from a 5-round internal magazine with a 29-inch barrel. This was the weapon carried by the vast majority of German infantry in 1913.

**Karabiner 98AZ:** In January 1908, the Karabiner Model 1898 AZ was accepted for service. It featured a small-diameter receiver ring, tapered barrel contour, an L-shaped stacking rod, and a turned-down bolt handle. It was issued to German cavalry, artillery, and various support units. Shorter and handier than the Gew 98, it fired the same 7.92×57mm cartridge from a 23-inch barrel.

**Gewehr 88 (Gew 88 / "Commission Rifle"):** By 1912, the Gewehr 98 had replaced all other rifles for the regular army and first-line reserve troops, but the Gew 88 remained in second-line and reserve stocks in 1913. It was an older 7.92mm bolt-action rifle, many units of which had been modified to accept the S Patrone cartridge.

### Machine Guns

**MG 08 (Maschinengewehr 08):** The MG 08 was a heavy machine gun developed from the Maxim gun, designed to provide sustained and accurate firepower to infantry units. Under optimal conditions it could fire 450 rounds per minute of 7.92×57mm Mauser ammunition with a muzzle velocity of 2,900 ft/sec. The ammunition was fed via a cloth belt on a 250-round strip. Right from the start of the war, a German army regiment was equipped with six MG 08s. At 58 lbs for the gun alone, it required a crew to operate.

### Pistols and Revolvers

**Luger P08 (Pistole Parabellum 1908):** In 1908, the German Army adopted the P08 chambered in 9×19mm Parabellum. The grip safety used on earlier versions was omitted, and a lug was attached to the heel of the pistol frame for attachment of a shoulder stock. This was the standard officer and NCO sidearm by 1913.

**Mauser C96 ("Broomhandle"):** There were a number of other handguns in service, including the Mauser C96. A semi-automatic pistol with a distinctive front-mounted integral magazine, it fired the 7.63×25mm Mauser cartridge and was privately purchased by many officers. Some were also issued with a wooden shoulder stock/holster combination.

**Reichsrevolver M1879 and M1883:** The M1879 Reichsrevolver was a service revolver used by the German Army from 1879 to 1908, when it was superseded by the Luger. Both models were single-action, solid-frame, non-ejecting six-shot revolvers chambered in 10.6×25mmR. Although the design was dated, the weapon was extremely robust, and they were still used through World War I. In 1913 they remained in rear-echelon, artillery, and reserve service.

**Dreyse M1907:** The Dreyse M1907 was among the handguns in German Army use alongside the Luger. It was a blowback-operated semi-automatic pistol in 7.65mm that saw limited military issue, primarily to some NCOs and officers as a supplemental sidearm.

**Mauser Model 1914:** A compact semi-automatic pocket pistol in 7.65mm produced by Mauser, carried by some officers as a personal sidearm. It entered production just as 1913 ended and was in the pipeline for wider officer use.

The picture in 1913 is one of transition. Owing to the great shortage of Lugers, as many as 28 different models of pistols and revolvers were ultimately in use in the German Army by the war years, meaning an enormous variety of privately purchased and supplemental handguns circulated among officers. The Gew 98 and MG 08 were the two weapons that truly defined what the German soldier carried into battle.

## Issue Ammunition by Weapon

Comprehensive overview of every bullet type tied to standard German Army weapons in 1913, organized by weapon and caliber.

### 7.92×57mm Mauser: Rifle and Machine Gun Cartridges

This was the primary infantry cartridge, used in the Gewehr 98 bolt-action rifle and the MG 08 machine gun.

**S Patrone (Spitzgeschoss, "Pointed Bullet"):** The 7.92×57mm Mauser S Patrone was introduced on 3 April 1903, replacing the older M/88 round. It was loaded with a new 8.20 mm (.323 in), 9.9 g (154 gr) spitzer bullet. This was the standard-issue ball round in 1913.

**Patrone 88 (M/88):** The M/88 cartridge was loaded with a relatively heavy, 14.7 g (227 gr), round-nosed ball bullet with a diameter of 8.08 mm (0.318 in), propelled by smokeless powder. Though largely superseded by the S Patrone, older stocks remained in limited use.

The 1903 pattern S Patrone was loaded with a lighter pointed spitzer bullet and more powerful double-base smokeless powder, resulting in nearly 38% higher muzzle velocity and 27% more muzzle energy than the M/88.

The 7.92×57mm family also existed in observed/tracer and armor-piercing variants being developed during this period, though the S Patrone was overwhelmingly the standard round in 1913.

### 9×19mm Parabellum: Pistol Cartridge

In 1908, the German Army adopted the P08 (Luger), chambered in 9×19mm Parabellum. The 9×19mm DWM cartridge initially adopted by the German Army featured a 123-grain truncated-nose bullet design intended to increase the wounding effect of the fully jacketed bullet. This was the primary pistol round for officers and NCOs carrying the Luger P08 by 1913.

### 10.6×25mmR: Reichsrevolver Cartridge

The Reichsrevolver M1879 and M1883 revolvers fired this older, large-bore blackpowder-era rimmed revolver cartridge. The Luger P08 replaced the Reichsrevolver as the German front-line service handgun in 1908, but Reichsrevolvers were still carried by some rear-echelon troops and reservists in 1913, making this cartridge still present in the inventory.

### 7.65mm Parabellum

Some earlier Luger variants and certain pistols carried by cavalry officers fired the 7.65×21mm Parabellum cartridge, the predecessor to the 9mm. The first Luger pistols adopted by Switzerland were chambered in 7.65mm Parabellum, and this round remained in some circulation.

### Issue Ammunition Summary Table

| Cartridge | Weapon | Use |
|---|---|---|
| 7.92×57mm S Patrone | Gewehr 98, MG 08 | Standard infantry rifle/MG round |
| 7.92×57mm M/88 | Older Gewehr 88 rifles | Legacy/reserve stocks |

The 7.92×57mm S Patrone utterly dominated the German infantryman's kit in 1913. The Mauser Gewehr 98 rifle fired 7.92mm cartridges from a five-round magazine, and the MG 08 machine gun used the same round fed via 250-round canvas belts. Everything else was a sidearm caliber.

## Complete Ammunition Catalog

Every cartridge a German soldier could purchase or use in Germany in 1913, spanning military-issue rounds, commercial sporting ammunition, pistol cartridges, rimfire rounds, and imported foreign calibers.

### Part I: Military Service Cartridges

These were the rounds issued by the German Imperial Army and Navy. They could also be purchased commercially or obtained from military surplus.

#### 7.92×57mm Mauser: S Patrone (Standard Ball)

- **Adopted:** 1903 to 1905, replacing the M/88
- **Bullet:** 8.20 mm (.323 in) diameter, 9.9 g (154 gr) full-metal-jacket spitzer (pointed)
- **Propellant:** Double-base smokeless powder (nitrocellulose + nitroglycerin)
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~880 m/s (2,887 ft/s) from Gewehr 98
- **Weapons:** Gewehr 98, Karabiner 98AZ, MG 08
- **Notes:** The dominant round of the German military. Loaded in 5-round stripper clips for rifles, 250-round canvas belts for the MG 08. By 1913 this was the universal standard cartridge; the vast majority of German infantry training and combat doctrine revolved around it. Also widely sold commercially.

#### 7.92×57mm Mauser: M/88 (Patrone 88, Round-Nose Ball)

- **Adopted:** 1888
- **Bullet:** 8.08 mm (.318 in) diameter, 14.7 g (227 gr) full-metal-jacket round-nose ("J bore")
- **Propellant:** Single-base smokeless powder (nitrocellulose)
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~620 m/s (2,034 ft/s)
- **Weapons:** Gewehr 88 ("Commission Rifle"), early Gewehr 98 rifles chambered for it
- **Notes:** Officially superseded by the S Patrone, but enormous stockpiles remained. Still issued to second-line, reserve, and colonial troops in 1913. Important: the M/88 round uses a .318" bullet; the later S Patrone uses .323". Rifles chambered for one should not use the other. A distinction not always observed in the field.

#### 7.92×57mm Mauser: Beobachtungs-Patrone (Observation/Tracer Bullet)

- **Bullet:** Special composition for observing bullet fall
- **Notes:** Experimental and limited-production by 1913; more developed during WWI. Existed in German service inventories in prototype/trial quantities.

#### 9×19mm Parabellum: DWM Catalog No. 278F (Standard Military Pistol Load)

- **Adopted:** 1908 (with P08 Luger)
- **Bullet:** 7.45 g (115 gr) or 8.0 g (123 gr) full-metal-jacket truncated-nose or round-nose
- **Propellant:** Smokeless powder
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~360 m/s (1,181 ft/s)
- **Weapons:** Luger P08 (Pistole Parabellum 1908)
- **Notes:** The standard officer and NCO sidearm cartridge from 1908. The initial military loading used a truncated-nose bullet to improve wounding effect within the constraints of the Hague Convention's full-metal-jacket requirement. Both 7-round standard magazines and experimental 32-round "snail drum" (Trommelmagazin 08) were loaded with this cartridge. The drum magazine was authorized for the Artillery Luger (Lange Pistole 08) in July 1913.

#### 10.6×25mmR: Reichsrevolver (Military Revolver Cartridge)

- **Adopted:** 1879
- **Bullet:** ~17.5 g (270 gr) lead, round-nose
- **Propellant:** Black powder (original loadings); some later smokeless conversions existed commercially
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~200 m/s (~660 ft/s)
- **Weapons:** Reichsrevolver M1879, Reichsrevolver M1883
- **Notes:** The Reichsrevolver was officially replaced by the Luger P08 in 1908, but revolvers remained in service with rear-echelon, artillery, supply, and reserve troops in 1913. Surplus revolvers and their cartridges were still available commercially. A single-action 6-shot revolver cartridge. Sometimes compared in power to the contemporary .44 Russian.

### Part II: Pistol Cartridges (Military Issue and Widely Carried)

German officers and NCOs frequently carried privately purchased sidearms in addition to, or instead of, issue weapons. The following cartridges were commercially available in Germany in 1913.

#### 7.65×21mm Parabellum (.30 Luger)

- **Bullet:** ~5.5 g (85 gr) full-metal-jacket round-nose or truncated
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~380 m/s (1,247 ft/s)
- **Weapons:** Early Luger Model 1900 (Swiss contract), Luger Model 1902, some commercial Luger variants
- **Notes:** The original Parabellum cartridge developed by Georg Luger ca. 1898, derived by necking down the 7.65mm Borchardt case. The German military standardized on 9mm Parabellum in 1908, but 7.65mm Luger pistols remained in private ownership and the cartridge was still manufactured and sold by DWM and RWS.

#### 7.63×25mm Mauser

- **Bullet:** ~5.5 g (85 gr) full-metal-jacket bottlenecked, high-velocity
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~430 m/s (1,410 ft/s)
- **Weapons:** Mauser C96 "Broomhandle" pistol
- **Notes:** The Mauser C96 was not an official German Army issue weapon but was extremely popular among German officers and colonial forces. The 7.63×25mm Mauser cartridge was one of the highest-velocity pistol rounds available in 1913. DWM (Case Number 403) was the primary manufacturer. The C96 was also offered in 9mm Parabellum (with a red "9" carved in the grip) though this was more common later.

#### 6.35×15mmSR (.25 ACP / 6.35mm Browning)

- **Bullet:** ~3.2 g (50 gr) full-metal-jacket
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~230 m/s (755 ft/s)
- **Weapons:** FN Model 1906 pocket pistol, various small German and Belgian commercial pocket pistols
- **Notes:** The .25 ACP was introduced by Browning/FN in 1905 and rapidly spread across Europe. By 1913 it was a common cartridge for small pocket pistols widely sold in Germany. DWM produced it (Case No. 513).

#### 7.65×17mmSR (.32 ACP / 7.65mm Browning)

- **Bullet:** ~4.7 g (73 gr) full-metal-jacket
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~300 m/s (984 ft/s)
- **Weapons:** Dreyse M1907, FN Model 1900, FN Model 1910, Mauser Model 1910, Savage Model 1907 (imported), and many commercial pocket pistols
- **Notes:** Introduced by John Browning in 1899, the .32 ACP had become one of the most common pistol cartridges in Europe by 1913. Widely manufactured by DWM, RWS, and imported from Belgium (FN). The Dreyse M1907 was officially carried by some German NCOs and officers.

#### 9×17mm (.380 ACP / 9mm Browning Short / 9mm Kurz)

- **Bullet:** ~6.0 g (95 gr) full-metal-jacket
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~290 m/s (951 ft/s)
- **Weapons:** FN Model 1910, various European pocket pistols
- **Notes:** Introduced by Browning in 1908 and available in Germany commercially by 1910 to 1912. Gaining popularity by 1913 but not yet common in German military use. DWM catalogued it. Later became the cartridge for the Walther PP (1929).

#### 9×23mm Steyr (9mm Steyr)

- **Bullet:** ~8.0 g (123 gr) full-metal-jacket
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~375 m/s (1,230 ft/s)
- **Weapons:** Steyr-Hahn M1912 (Austro-Hungarian service pistol)
- **Notes:** The Steyr M1912 was adopted by Austria-Hungary in 1912 and its cartridge was available in Germany commercially. German officers with an interest in Austrian sidearms, or stationed near the Austrian border, could access this ammunition.

#### 9mm Bergmann-Bayard (9×23mm Largo)

- **Bullet:** ~8.0 g (123 gr) full-metal-jacket
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~365 m/s (1,197 ft/s)
- **Weapons:** Bergmann-Bayard pistol (adopted by Denmark and Spain); also the German Bergmann Mars pistol
- **Notes:** Available commercially in Germany as an early high-power pistol cartridge.

### Part III: German Sporting and Hunting Rifle Cartridges

Germany had a rich tradition of Büchsenmacher (gunsmiths) and a large hunting culture. The following cartridges were commercially available in Germany in 1913, manufactured chiefly by DWM (Deutsche Waffen- und Munitionsfabriken) in Karlsruhe/Berlin, RWS (Rheinisch-Westfälische Sprengstoff) in Nürnberg, and the many Suhl-area ammo makers.

#### 8×57mm J / 8×57mm JS (Commercial Mauser)

- **Bullet:** J-bore: .318" (8.08mm); JS-bore: .323" (8.20mm)
- **Notes:** The commercial sporting designation for the military 7.92×57mm round. Widely used in hunting sporters built on Mauser 98 actions. **These are two different chamberings:** J for the old .318" bore, JS (Spitzgeschoss) for the new .323" bore. Many German hunters in 1913 had rifles in both variants. The rimmed variant **8×57mmIR/JR** was also produced for use in double rifles and drillings.

#### 8×57mmIR / 8×57mmJR (Rimmed Mauser: Double Rifles and Drillings)

- **Notes:** The rimmed version of the above, designed for use in break-action double rifles (Doppelbüchsen) and drilling combination guns. Widely stocked.

#### 7×57mm Mauser

- **Bullet:** 9.1 to 11.0 g (140 to 170 gr), various sporting loads
- **Notes:** Originally the Spanish military cartridge of 1892; widely adopted across German sporting use by 1913. Available from DWM, RWS, and others. Popular in Africa and among German colonial hunters. Also available in a rimmed variant (7×57mmR) for drillings. Mauser produced commercial Type C, S, and M sporter rifles in this caliber before WWI.

#### 9.3×62mm

- **Introduced:** 1905 by Otto Bock
- **Bullet:** 18.5 to 19.3 g (285 to 300 gr)
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~750 m/s (2,461 ft/s)
- **Notes:** The workhorse cartridge of German colonial hunters in Africa by 1913. Designed for use in inexpensive Mauser 98 bolt-action sporters. Already well-established in German East Africa and German Southwest Africa. Manufactured by DWM and RWS. Standard in Mauser Type A and Type B sporters.

#### 9.3×57mm

- **Bullet:** ~18 to 19 g (280 to 295 gr)
- **Notes:** An earlier rimless 9.3mm cartridge, predating the 9.3×62mm. Used in Mauser 98 sporters with slightly reduced performance compared to the 9.3×62mm. Available from German ammo makers.

#### 9.3×72mmR (Rimmed)

- **Notes:** An older rimmed 9.3mm cartridge for use in single-shot and break-action hunting rifles (Kipplaufbüchsen). Still in production and use in 1913 for older German hunting guns.

#### 6.5×54mm Mannlicher-Schönauer

- **Bullet:** ~9.0 g (139 gr)
- **Notes:** Developed for the Greek Mannlicher-Schönauer rifle (1903) and popular in continental European sporting use. Available from DWM. Listed in Mauser sporter catalogs (as the 6.5×54mm Mauser K, dimensionally near-identical).

#### 6.5×55mm Swedish Mauser

- **Bullet:** ~9.0 g (139 gr)
- **Notes:** Adopted jointly by Sweden and Norway in 1894. Available commercially in Germany by 1913 via DWM.

#### 6.5×57mm Mauser

- **Notes:** A commercially available rimless cartridge in 6.5mm on the 57mm Mauser case, offered in German sporting rifles. Available from DWM.

#### 6.5×57mmR (Rimmed)

- **Notes:** The rimmed version of the above, for use in drillings and break-action rifles.

#### 6.5×58mm Portugiesisch (Portuguese Vergueiro)

- **Notes:** Available commercially; some Mauser commercial sporters were chambered in it.

#### 8.15×46mmR: Schützen Target Cartridge

- **Introduced:** ~1893 to 1894 by Adolf Frohn of Suhl; standardized 1909 to 1910
- **Bullet:** ~8.5 to 9.0 g (130 to 140 gr) cast lead, single-driving-band
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~450 to 500 m/s
- **Notes:** By 1910 this was the dominant cartridge for German Schützen target shooting: precision standing-position rifle competition at 100 m, 175 m, and occasionally 300 m. In 1909 a committee standardized its dimensions, with the word "Norm" or "Normal" added to headstamps. Manufactured by RWS, DWM, and many Suhl-area firms. Also loaded in light hunting versions. Conceptually similar to the American .32-40 Remington-Winches.

#### 10.75×68mm Mauser

- **Bullet:** ~22 to 26 g (340 to 400 gr)
- **Notes:** A large-bore express cartridge developed for Mauser Type A sporters for dangerous game hunting in Africa and Asia. Available from DWM. Not suitable for carbines due to its heavy powder charge. The top of the Mauser commercial sporting line in 1913.

#### 11×60mmR Mauser (.43 Mauser)

- **Introduced:** 1871
- **Bullet:** ~25 g (385 gr) lead, black-powder era
- **Weapons:** Mauser Model 1871 infantry rifle; also used in commercial single-shot hunting rifles
- **Notes:** Long obsolete for military use, but still present in German sporting culture in 1913 in older single-shot hunting rifles. Ammunition was still available from German manufacturers, though declining. A heavy black-powder round from the era of the Reichsgründung.

#### 8×75mmRS (Rimmed Semi, or "8×75 S")

- **Notes:** Available in Germany as a high-performance cartridge for heavy game, used in some double rifles.

#### 5.6×35mmR (Vierling / Gallery Cartridge)

- **Notes:** Small-caliber German gallery and small-game cartridge in rimmed form. Produced by RWS and Suhl manufacturers.

#### 5.6×52mmR (.22 Savage Hi-Power)

- **Introduced:** 1912 by Savage Arms (USA)
- **Notes:** This fast-stepping small-bore American cartridge was imported and available in Germany by 1912 to 1913, chambered in some German bolt-action sporters.

### Part IV: Schützenpulver / Target Pistol Cartridges

German precision pistol shooting (Zimmerstutzen and Schützenpistole culture) supported a range of dedicated target pistol cartridges.

#### 4mm Zimmerpatrone (4mm Gallery Round)

- **Notes:** An extremely small indoor gallery cartridge, essentially a primed case with a tiny lead ball, used in Zimmerstutzen (parlor/gallery rifles and pistols) for indoor target shooting. Still produced by RWS in Nürnberg in 1913.

#### 6mm Flobert / .22 BB Cap

- **Introduced:** 1845 (Flobert); .22 CB Cap introduced 1888
- **Notes:** The classic European parlor and gallery cartridge. No powder: propelled solely by the percussion cap. Used in Salon-Pistolen (parlor pistols), gallery rifles, and target pistols. The .22 CB Cap (slightly longer, with a 20 gr conical bullet) was interchangeably called "6mm Flobert" in Germany. Produced by RWS (H. Utendörfer, Nürnberg) in tins of 100 and 250. Still very widely used in Germany in 1913 for indoor shooting clubs.

#### 9mm Flobert (Garden Gun / Pest Control)

- **Notes:** A smooth-bore shotshell cartridge in rimfire format, firing a small charge of shot. Used in garden guns (Gartengewehre) for bird and pest control on farms. Available from German manufacturers.

### Part V: Rimfire Cartridges

Germany had a rich rimfire shooting tradition. All of the following were commercially available in 1913, manufactured chiefly by RWS and DWM.

#### .22 Short (.22 kurz)

- **Introduced:** 1857 (USA)
- **Bullet:** ~1.9 g (29 gr) lead round-nose
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~330 m/s (1,083 ft/s)
- **Notes:** A universally available small-game and target cartridge by 1913. Used in revolvers, gallery pistols, and small training rifles. European production was standard by the 1900s.

#### .22 Long

- **Introduced:** 1871 (USA)
- **Bullet:** ~2.4 g (37 gr)
- **Notes:** Available but less common than .22 Short or .22 Long Rifle. A transitional cartridge.

#### .22 Long Rifle (.22 lfB / .22 lang für Büchsen)

- **Introduced:** 1887 (USA, J. Stevens Arms)
- **Bullet:** ~2.6 g (40 gr) lead round-nose
- **Muzzle velocity:** ~380 m/s (1,247 ft/s)
- **Notes:** By 1913 becoming the dominant small-bore rifle and pistol cartridge for target shooting and small game. DWM and RWS both produced it. Increasingly popular in German marksmanship clubs for training.

#### .22 Winchester Automatic

- **Notes:** A cartridge chambered in the Winchester Model 1903 self-loading rifle, available in Germany as an import for those who owned the rifle.

### Part VI: Imported Foreign Cartridges Available in Germany

Germany in 1913 had a vigorous arms trade and open borders (for sporting purposes) with most of Europe. The following foreign cartridges were obtainable through German sporting goods dealers, importers, and gunsmiths.

#### .303 British

- **Bullet:** ~13 g (200 gr) full-metal-jacket
- **Notes:** British military and sporting cartridge, available in Germany through importers. Some German hunters who owned Lee-Enfield or British sporting rifles could obtain it from DWM or import.

#### 7.65×53mm Argentine Mauser (7.65mm Belgian / .30 Argentine)

- **Notes:** Used in Argentine and Belgian military Mausers. Available commercially from DWM, which manufactured it for export.

#### 6.5×52mm Mannlicher-Carcano (Italian military)

- **Notes:** Available commercially, though uncommon in Germany.

#### 6.5×50mmSR Arisaka (Japanese military)

- **Notes:** Available via DWM, which held Japanese military production contracts. Rare in civilian German use.

#### 7.62×54mmR Mosin-Nagant (Russian military)

- **Notes:** Available commercially from DWM and through German exporters who supplied Russian contracts. German gunsmiths near the Russian border could obtain it.

#### .30-06 Springfield (7.62×63mm)

- **Notes:** The American military cartridge adopted in 1906. Available through importers and sporting goods dealers in Germany by 1913, particularly for those with American sporting rifles. DWM loaded it commercially.

#### .30-03 Springfield

- **Notes:** The predecessor to the .30-06 (adopted 1903, replaced 1906). Available from older American stocks in Germany, though being superseded.

#### 8mm Lebel (8×50mmR Lebel: French military)

- **Notes:** The French military cartridge since 1886. Available commercially in Germany through DWM, which also produced it. Some German hunters owned Lebel-chambered rifles.

#### 7.5×55mm Swiss (GP90, GP11)

- **Notes:** The Swiss military cartridge. Available commercially in Germany given the close economic ties with Switzerland.

#### 7×57mm Mauser (Spanish / International)

See Part III. Also an international cartridge used by Spain, Mexico, South Africa, and others. Widely available in Germany as a sporting round.

#### .318 Westley Richards (8.05×65mm)

- **Notes:** A British sporting cartridge available in Germany; some Mauser sporters were chambered for it. DWM produced it.

#### .404 Jeffery (10.75×73mm)

- **Notes:** A powerful British big-game cartridge. Some Mauser Type A sporters were chambered for it. Available from importers.

#### .375 Holland and Holland Belted Magnum

- **Introduced:** 1912 by Holland and Holland
- **Notes:** Just entering the market in 1912 to 1913. Available through import from London. An unusual find in Germany by 1913 but obtainable by affluent hunters.

#### .416 Rigby

- **Introduced:** 1911
- **Notes:** British big-bore sporting cartridge, available through import from Rigby in London for wealthy German African hunters.

#### .450 Nitro Express / .470 Nitro Express and other British Nitro Expresses

- **Notes:** British large-bore dangerous game cartridges for double rifles. Available through British importers in Germany (J. Rigby, Holland and Holland, Westley Richards). Primarily for colonial hunters returning to equip themselves.

### Part VIII: Summary Reference Table (Centerfire Metallic Cartridges)

| Cartridge | Type | Origin | Primary Use |

## Manufacturers Active in Germany in 1913

**Deutsche Waffen- und Munitionsfabriken (DWM):** Berlin and Karlsruhe. The largest German ammunition manufacturer. Produced military S Patrone, Parabellum pistol cartridges, and an enormous commercial line spanning essentially every metric rifle, pistol, and sporting cartridge. Used a numbered case catalog system (e.g., Case No. 278F for 9×19mm Parabellum, Case No. 403 for 7.63×25mm Mauser).

**RWS (H. Utendörfer / Rheinisch-Westfälische Sprengstoff):** Nürnberg. Founded 1886. Major producer of rimfire, target, and sporting ammunition. Produced 4mm and 6mm Flobert gallery rounds, .22 rimfire, 8.15×46R Schützen ammunition, and a full line of hunting cartridges.

**Rottweil:** Rottweil am Neckar. Primarily known for shotshells and smokeless powders. A well-established brand for sporting use.

**Suhl-area firms:** Numerous small makers in Suhl, Thuringia (the "German Birmingham"), produced custom cartridges, target loads, and specialty rifle ammunition.
