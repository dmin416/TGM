# Absorption Routes: Complete Guide

## History of Inhalers

### Before the 1900s
- Smoke inhalation (opium, cannabis) for systemic effects
- Vapor inhalation (ether, chloroform) for anesthesia
- Simple atomizers for perfume and liquids

### 1900s to 1950s
- Hand-held squeeze nebulizers (epinephrine for asthma, ~1900s)
- Metered-dose inhalers (MDIs) developed (~1956): pressurized canister with propellant
- Bronchodilators (albuterol/salbutamol) became standard

### 1960s to 1980s
- Dry powder inhalers (DPIs): patient inhales to activate drug dispersion
- Combination therapies (bronchodilators + corticosteroids)
- Better particle size control and aerosol science

### 1990s to present
- Nebulizers refined for hospital/home use (vibrating mesh nebulizers)
- Smart inhalers with dose counters and Bluetooth connectivity
- Experimental: insulin inhalers (Afrezza approved 2014), vaccine inhalers, biologics

## Lung Absorption (Respiratory Route)

### Particle Deposition Zones

**Oropharyngeal (>10 μm):** Trapped in mouth/throat, swallowed (enters GI)
**Tracheobronchial (5-10 μm):** Mid-airways, cleared by mucociliary clearance (24-48 hours)
**Alveolar (<5 μm):** Deep lung, slow clearance (days to weeks), best absorption

### What Lungs Absorb Well

**Small lipophilic molecules (<500 Da):**
- Oxygen, CO₂ (respiratory gases)
- Anesthetics (ether, isoflurane, sevoflurane)
- Volatile drugs (salbutamol ~239 Da, albuterol ~239 Da)
- Nicotine (162 Da): absorbed rapidly, 80-90% bioavailability
- Cannabis compounds (THC ~314 Da, CBD ~314 Da)

**Particles that deposit and absorb:**
- Corticosteroids (fluticasone ~500 Da, beclomethasone ~408 Da)
- Lactose particles (inert carrier, eventually cleared by alveolar macrophages)
- Peptides/proteins (<5 μm particle size, ~1,000-10,000 Da)

**Approved inhaled biologics:**
- Insulin (Afrezza): ~5,800 Da, 51 amino acids, particle size 1-3 μm
- Tobramycin (antibiotic for cystic fibrosis lung infections)
- Dornase alfa (enzyme, DNase, breaks down sputum)

**Bioavailability:** 10-20% typical; **lung bypass first-pass metabolism** (major advantage over GI)

**Absorption speed:** Seconds to minutes (ether anesthesia works in 3-5 minutes; nicotine in under 1 minute)

### What Lungs DON'T Absorb

**Too large:**
- Intact proteins >20,000 Da: cleared by alveolar macrophages
- Whole monoclonal antibodies (~150,000 Da): physically too large to cross alveolar epithelium
- Viruses as intact particles: immune system clears before absorption

**Enzyme degradation:**
- Unprotected peptides/proteins: lung has proteases (trypsin-like, elastase)
- Most proteins degraded before absorption (exception: insulin with special formulation)

**Stability issues:**
- Acid-unstable compounds: lung pH is ~7.4, relatively neutral but oxidative environment
- Compounds sensitive to proteolytic enzymes
- Highly hydrophilic (water-loving) compounds: lipid membrane barrier

**Clearance barriers:**
- Mucociliary clearance: mucus blanket moves particles up and out in 24-48 hours
- Alveolar macrophages: engulf foreign particles, especially >5 μm
- Highly charged molecules: stick to mucus, don't cross epithelium

### Lung Disease Effects on Absorption

- **COPD/Emphysema:** Reduced surface area, slower absorption
- **Cystic fibrosis:** Thick mucus blocks absorption
- **Pulmonary edema:** Fluid floods alveoli, prevents drug deposition
- **Asthma:** Bronchoconstriction reduces deep lung penetration

## Nasal/Rhinopharyngeal Absorption

### Advantages Over Other Routes

- Peptides/proteins absorb better than GI (avoids acid, pepsin, proteases)
- Rich immune tissue (nasopharyngeal-associated lymphoid tissue, NALT)
- Large surface area with high blood flow
- Bypasses first-pass hepatic metabolism
- Better bioavailability than oral for many compounds

### What Absorbs Well Nasally

**Hormones/peptides:**
- Desmopressin (diabetes insipidus treatment)
- Oxytocin (research/clinical use)
- LHRH agonists (reproductive disorders)
- Calcitonin (bone disorders)

**Opioids:**
- Fentanyl nasal spray (breakthrough pain)
- Butorphanol nasal spray

**Vaccines:**
- FluMist (live attenuated influenza, nasal): approval suspended 2016, re-approved 2024
- Intranasal COVID-19 vaccines (in development/trials, some approved in select countries)
- Intranasal polio vaccine (OPV): widely used globally

**Small molecules:**
- Antihistamines (rapid onset)
- Decongestants (oxymetazoline)
- Anticonvulsants (midazolam nasal for seizures)

**Bioavailability:** 40-90% (dramatically better than GI for peptides/proteins)

**Absorption speed:** 10-30 minutes

### What Doesn't Work Nasally

- Large proteins without mucosal enhancers
- Highly unstable in nasal mucus (proteases present)
- Compounds requiring first-pass metabolism (need liver processing)

## Skin Absorption (Dermal/Transdermal)

### Skin as a Barrier

Stratum corneum (outermost layer) is lipid-rich, blocks hydrophilic compounds. Only small lipophilic molecules reliably cross.

### Readily Absorbed

**Small lipophilic molecules (<500 Da):**
- Nicotine patches: excellent penetration, 80%+ absorption over 16-24 hours
- Fentanyl patches: systemic opioid delivery, 72-hour patches
- Estradiol patches: hormone replacement
- Testosterone patches/gels: HRT
- Nitroglycerine patches: angina relief

**Organic solvents/volatile compounds:**
- DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide): penetration enhancer, itself absorbable

**Salicylates:**
- Aspirin derivatives (methyl salicylate in liniments)

**Bioavailability:** 5-10% for most compounds (skin is an excellent barrier)

### Can Penetrate with Enhancers

- Limonene (citrus extract): permeation enhancer
- Surfactants: break down lipid bilayers
- Propylene glycol: solvent enhancer
- Oleic acid: natural enhancer

### Poor Dermal Absorption

- Water-soluble compounds (glucose, salts, electrolytes)
- Large proteins/peptides (insulin, antibodies): physically cannot cross
- Highly charged molecules: interact with skin lipids poorly
- Anything >500 Da without special formulation/enhancers

### Damaged Skin

- Burned skin, wounds, eczema: dramatically increased absorption (10-100× higher)
- Reason: barrier function compromised

## Ocular Absorption (Eye Drops)

### Excellent Local Absorption

**Anesthetics:**
- Lidocaine eye drops (topical anesthesia)
- Tetracaine (longer-acting)

**Antibiotics:**
- Tobramycin
- Ciprofloxacin
- Gentamicin
- Ofloxacin

**Antihistamines:**
- Ketotifen (itching relief)
- Azelastine

**Steroids:**
- Dexamethasone
- Prednisolone acetate
- Fluorometholone

**Antiglaucoma agents:**
- Latanoprost (prostaglandin analog)
- Timolol (beta-blocker)
- Pilocarpine (cholinergic)

**Bioavailability (topical/local):** High for local corneal/intraocular effect

### Systemic Absorption from Eyes

- Rich blood supply (orbital veins → systemic circulation, bypass liver)
- Tears wash away most drug quickly (1-3 minute residence time)
- Some compounds achieve significant systemic levels

**Example:** Beta-blocker eye drops (timolol) can cause systemic bradycardia, respiratory depression in sensitive patients

**Bioavailability (systemic):** 1-3% typically (tears wash most away)

### What Doesn't Work

- Large molecules (proteins, antibodies)
- Compounds that don't dissolve in aqueous tear film
- Highly charged molecules (stick to mucus, wash away)

## Buccal/Sublingual Absorption

### Route Characteristics

- Bypasses first-pass metabolism (blood drains to systemic circulation via facial veins)
- Rich blood supply, permeable mucosa
- Small volume, limited for large doses
- Tablets dissolve under tongue or in cheek pouch

### What Works

**Nitroglycerin (nitrates):**
- Sublingual tablets for angina: works in 2-3 minutes
- Much faster than swallowing (GI absorption slower)

**Opioids:**
- Fentanyl buccal tablets (breakthrough pain)
- Buprenorphine sublingual (opioid substitution therapy)

**Benzodiazepines:**
- Midazolam sublingual (anxiety, seizures)

**Corticosteroids:**
- Some steroids absorbed sublingually faster

**Bioavailability:** 30-80% (avoids first-pass, but limited by mucosal area)

**Absorption speed:** 2-10 minutes

### Limitations

- Small mucosal surface area (limits dose)
- Requires staying in mouth (patient compliance)
- Some compounds irritate mucosa

---

## Rectal Absorption

### Route Characteristics

- Portal vs. systemic venous drainage (proximal rectum drains to portal vein ~50%, systemic ~50%; distal rectum drains systemic, bypassing liver)
- Rich blood supply
- Good for patients unable to take oral (sedated, vomiting, etc.)

### What Works

**Diclofenac (NSAID):**
- Suppository form for pain relief
- Bioavailability 50-60%

**Acetaminophen:**
- Rectal suppositories
- Bioavailability 80%+

**Corticosteroids:**
- Mesalamine enemas (inflammatory bowel disease)
- Hydrocortisone suppositories

**Benzodiazepines:**
- Diazepam suppositories (seizures, anxiety)

**Bioavailability:** 60-90% (comparable to oral, sometimes better)

**Absorption speed:** 15-45 minutes

### Limitations

- Patient acceptance issues
- Rectal disease/inflammation affects absorption
- Limited to solid formulations (suppositories)

## GI Tract Absorption (Oral/Esophageal)

### Esophagus

- **Mostly transient:** just a transport tube
- Minimal absorption under normal conditions
- Drugs can get stuck here (bisphosphonates, aspirin can cause ulceration)
- Sublingual/buccal absorption better for upper GI effect

### Stomach

- **pH 1.5-3.5** (highly acidic)
- Proteins degraded by pepsin
- Some absorption occurs but limited
- Empty stomach: faster transit (20-40 minutes); full stomach: slower (1-4 hours)

### Small Intestine (PRIMARY ABSORPTION SITE)

**Why it's the main site:**
- Enormous surface area (villi, microvilli, brush border): ~300 m² (size of tennis court)
- High blood flow (digestive blood supply is massive)
- Long transit time (3-4 hours) = more absorption time
- pH more neutral (6-7)
- Rich enzyme systems for active transport

**Excellent GI absorption:**
- Glucose, amino acids (active transport carriers)
- Lipids (special chylomicron absorption)
- Small hydrophilic organic compounds
- Most therapeutic drugs (aspirin, ibuprofen, metformin)
- Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K): require dietary fat for absorption
- Water-soluble vitamins (B, C): active & passive transport

**Bioavailability:** 80-95% for most drugs

### Colon

- Slower absorption than small intestine
- Some compounds designed for colon release (5-ASA compounds for ulcerative colitis)
- Water reabsorption: concentrated drug reservoir
- Bacterial metabolism can alter drugs

**Bioavailability:** 60-80% typically

### First-Pass Metabolism (Major Limitation)

- Absorbed drugs go to liver via hepatic portal vein **before systemic circulation**
- Liver enzymes (CYP450 system) metabolize/deactivate many drugs
- Some drugs completely inactivated in liver (nitroglycerin, testosterone: why patches/sublingual preferred)
- Reason why **insulin cannot be taken orally** (peptide broken down before reaching liver)

### What Doesn't Absorb in GI

**Proteins/peptides:**
- Insulin: degraded by pepsin in stomach
- Most biologics: broken down by proteases
- Antibodies: destroyed by stomach acid and enzymes
- Exceptions: Some peptides absorbed if protected (Exenatide, GLP-1 agonist: still <5% bioavailability)

**Highly lipophilic compounds:**
- Some oils/fats require bile for absorption
- Cannabis oil (lipophilic): absorbed but variable, food-dependent

**Highly charged molecules:**
- Electrolytes (sodium, potassium) absorbed but need active transport
- Most ions absorbed in distal small intestine/colon

### Food Interactions

- **Fat-soluble drugs:** Absorption increased with fatty meal
- **Grapefruit juice:** Inhibits CYP3A4, increases absorption of some drugs (statins, calcium-channel blockers)
- **Calcium/iron:** Reduce absorption of some antibiotics/levothyroxine
- **Alcohol:** Increases absorption of some drugs, decreases others

## Injection Routes

### Intravenous (IV)

- **100% bioavailability** (drug directly in bloodstream)
- Immediate effect (seconds to minutes)
- Most precise dosing
- Risk: immediate systemic exposure (no buffer)

### Intramuscular (IM)

- Rich blood supply in muscle
- **Bioavailability:** 75-100% depending on compound
- Onset: 5-30 minutes depending on drug
- Depot formulations: slow release over weeks/months

### Subcutaneous (SC)

- Slower absorption than IM
- **Bioavailability:** 60-100%
- Used for insulin, biologics, vaccines
- Onset: 15 minutes to hours

### Intradermal

- Very slow absorption
- Used mostly for vaccines (BCG, intradermal flu vaccine)
- Triggers local immune response

## Key Principles

### Molecular Weight Matters

- **<500 Da:** Generally absorb easily across most membranes
- **500-5,000 Da:** Variable; peptides need special formulation
- **5,000-20,000 Da:** Mostly blocked; requires special delivery (nanoparticles, liposomes)
- **>20,000 Da:** Proteins/antibodies cannot cross intact; must be injected

### Lipophilicity vs. Hydrophilicity

- **Lipophilic:** Cross lipid membranes easily; poor in aqueous environments (lungs, GI with mucus)
- **Hydrophilic:** Need active transport carriers; get stuck in mucus/fats
- **Sweet spot:** Moderate lipophilicity (some water solubility, some lipid solubility)

### pH Sensitivity

- Stomach acid (pH 1.5-3.5) destroys many peptides, proteins, sensitive compounds
- Duodenum/small intestine (pH 6-7) much gentler
- Lungs (pH 7.4) relatively neutral but oxidative

### Enzyme Degradation

- **Lungs:** Proteases, peptidases
- **Stomach:** Pepsin, gastric lipase
- **Small intestine:** Trypsin, chymotrypsin, pancreatic enzymes
- **Blood/tissues:** Esterases, peptidases, proteases
- **Liver:** CYP450 system (major metabolizer)

### Surface Area

- Small intestine >> colon > stomach > mouth/esophagus
- Lung alveoli have massive surface area but slower absorption
- Why small intestine is absorption champion

### Blood Flow

- **Highest:** Lungs, liver, kidney (perfusion-limited absorption)
- **High:** Small intestine, rectum
- **Moderate:** Skin, muscle
- **Low:** Esophagus, colon

## Special Formulation Strategies to Improve Absorption

### Peptide/Protein Delivery

- **Nanoparticles:** Encapsulate peptides to protect from enzymes
- **Liposomes:** Lipid vesicles carry hydrophilic drugs
- **Microparticles:** Insulin microparticles for inhalation (Afrezza)
- **Enhancers:** SNAC (sodium caprate), chitosan, aprotinin protease inhibitor
- **Coating:** Acid-resistant coatings protect from stomach
- **Conjugation:** Link to larger carrier proteins to avoid filtering by kidneys

### Transdermal Patches

- **Permeation enhancers:** DMSO, alcohols, essential oils
- **Penetration accelerators:** Azone, surfactants
- **Supersaturation:** Overly concentrated patch increases gradient
- **Occlusion:** Covered patch increases hydration, absorption

### Mucosal (Nasal/Oral/Rectal) Enhancement

- **Bioadhesives:** Chitosan, carbomers: stick to mucosa longer
- **Tight junction openers:** Zonula occludens toxin (ZOT), dialyzable components
- **Protease inhibitors:** Block enzyme degradation (aprotinin, leupeptin)
- **Surfactants:** Enhance paracellular transport

### Pulmonary Delivery

- **Particle size control:** Precise 1-5 μm for deep lung deposition
- **Excipient selection:** Mannitol, lactose as carriers
- **Dry powder:** More stable than liquid nebulizers
- **Coating:** Lipid coating protects peptides from lung proteases
