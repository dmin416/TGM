# Privilege, Pageantry, and Practice: Special Allowances, Privileges, and Exceptions in the Imperial German Army (Deutsches Heer), 1914

## TL;DR

- **The gap between regulation and reality was enormous and structural.** The Deutsches Heer of 1914 ran on a *written* rationing, uniform, and command code grounded in Prussian thrift and aristocratic decorum, but in practice officers, especially Guards, cavalry, and General Staff officers, lived under a parallel, privately-financed regime of tailor-made uniforms, private sidearms (Lugers, hunting rifles), personal orderlies (*Bursche/Putzer*), requisitioned châteaux, staff cars, and chateau-kept tables stocked from local wine cellars and home parcels. Enlisted men got the *Verpflegungssatz*; officers got a mess.
- **Two structural privileges underwrote everything else:** (1) The "one-year volunteer" (*Einjährig-Freiwilliger*) system that priced reserve-officer status at roughly 10,000 modern-Euro equivalent in self-paid kit, locking the corps to the propertied classes; and (2) *Auftragstaktik*, a doctrinally enshrined latitude that let junior commanders, down to company and even *Stoßtrupp* leader, substitute their own tactical judgment for orders. Both were peacetime privileges that became wartime force-multipliers.
- **The "biggest violations" were rarely punished because they served the institution.** Officers carried non-regulation Lugers, wore privately tailored *Litewkas* and tropical-grade boots, kept private cars and saddle horses at the front, hunted on Baltic estates while commanding Ober Ost, drank requisitioned French champagne in mess, and dueled in defiance of the Military Penal Code; the Kaiser, the regimental colonels, and the General Staff tolerated or quietly endorsed all of it as marks of *Standesehre* (estate honor).

## Key Findings

1. **Two food regimes, not one.** The official 1914 daily ration (*Verpflegungssatz I*, field) was 750 g bread, 375 g fresh meat, 1,500 g potatoes, coffee, sugar, salt, plus tobacco; and crucially, "**at the discretion of the commanding officer:** 0.17 pint spirits, 0.44 pint wine, 0.88 pint beer." That CO-discretion clause was the entire mechanism by which elite units, cavalry, and assault battalions ate and drank better than the line. *Sturm-Bataillone* received explicit **double rations** while in rear-area training between operations.
2. **The officer mess (*Kasino/Offizierheim*) was an institution legally separate from the troop kitchen.** Officers paid mess dues, dined apart, and were waited upon by enlisted *Ordonnanzen*. Wine, champagne, and spirits flowed at officers' tables, and 1914-1918-Online's archaeological work on Western Front trenches confirms "bottles of corked wine or oyster shells found during archaeological excavations" specifically at *officer* dugouts.
3. **The *Bursche*/orderly system was universal and unregulated in practice.** Every German officer was entitled to an enlisted orderly (officially *Ordonnanz*, colloquially *Bursche* or *Putzer*) who shaved him, polished his boots, prepared meals, opened parcels from home, and on campaign managed the officer's private baggage, duties that overlapped completely with civilian valet service.
4. **The officer corps was a self-selecting caste financed by private wealth.** The *Einjährig-Freiwilliger* program, the gateway to reserve commissions, required candidates to equip, clothe, and subsist themselves *entirely at their own cost*, which one modern estimate places at no less than €10,000 in today's money. Guard and cavalry regiments demanded an *additional* informal stipend; according to the historical reference site German1914, "**Guard and cavalry units could expect additional income of 1,000 Mark per month from these candidates who would pay to belong to the units.**"
5. **Auftragstaktik formalized a tactical privilege.** Mission-type tactics, coined as a derisive term by its critics, who preferred *Normaltaktik*, granted commanders at every level the right to deviate from the letter of orders to achieve the intent. The Prussian system actually went further: the chief of staff of any field formation had the formal right to disagree in writing with his commander's plan and appeal to the next-higher headquarters, an extraordinary check unique to the German army.
6. **Stoßtruppen received the army's biggest practical equipment and quality-of-life concessions.** Under *Hauptmann* Willy Rohr's *Sturm-Bataillon Nr. 5*, regulation jackboots were officially replaced by lighter Austrian-style mountain boots and *Gamaschen* (M1914 *Schnürschuhe*), uniforms got reinforced leather elbow/knee patches, the Stahlhelm was issued before the rest of the army, and the assault battalions ate **double rations** in their rear-training cycles.
7. **Private weapons were normal.** The Luger P08 was the official sidearm from 1908, but officers were expected to *purchase their own*, a practice with deep institutional roots: "Since 1900, some German officers had already been purchasing these pistols privately." Mauser C96 "Broomhandle" and Pistole 1914 (Mauser) were common private alternatives, and hunting rifles brought from home (especially in *Jäger* battalions, many of whose other ranks were former gamekeepers and poachers) were widespread.
8. **Cavalry quickly became a privileged anomaly.** The 110 peacetime cavalry regiments (26 Uhlan, plus hussars, dragoons, cuirassiers, *Chevaulegers*, and *Schwere Reiter*) were the most aristocratic, the most expensively uniformed, and (after autumn 1914) the most tactically obsolete arm. By war's end only 22 regiments remained mounted; the rest fought as *Kavallerie-Schützen* infantry. The Eastern Front, with its primitive battle conditions, was the *only* theater where horse cavalry retained its 1914 role for any length of time.
9. **Senior commanders lived in châteaux, fortresses, and command trains.** Hindenburg and Ludendorff established their joint Eastern command in the refurbished **citadel of Brest-Litovsk**, later moved to Kovno, traveled "on a staff train equipped with the most advanced communication apparatus," and Baltic German nobles "feted Hindenburg and he hunted their game preserves." Kaiser Wilhelm II's preferred staff car was the 1916 Mercedes 16/40 "Knight Tourer."
10. **The dueling code (*Ehrenkodex*) was illegal under the Military Penal Code and universally tolerated.** Wilhelm II's documented position was that he "would punish any officer who fought a duel, but would dismiss from the army any one who refused to do so," a perfect summary of the *Heer*'s habit of writing one rule and enforcing the opposite.

## Details

### 1. Food: Regulation vs. Practice

**The regulation (peacetime garrison and field, 1914):**

- *Verpflegungssatz I* (field, on campaign), daily: 750 g bread (or 500 g field biscuit / 400 g egg biscuit); 375 g fresh or frozen meat (or 200 g preserved); 1,500 g potatoes or 125–250 g fresh vegetables or 60 g dried vegetables; 25 g coffee or 3 g tea; 20 g sugar; 25 g salt; 2 cigars + 2 cigarettes (or pipe tobacco/snuff equivalents); **plus, at the discretion of the CO, 0.17 pint spirits, 0.44 pint wine, or 0.88 pint beer.**
- *Verpflegungssatz III* (garrison): roughly 700 g rye bread, 90 g meat-with-bone, smaller fat and sugar allowances, 3 cigarettes, and (in summer only) a small wine allowance.
- An "iron ration" (*eiserne Portion*: tinned meat, biscuit, salt, coffee) was carried by every soldier and could only be opened on an officer's order. In practice it was eaten without permission whenever supply broke down.

**The reality:**

- **Officer messes operated on a separate economy.** The *Offizierheimgesellschaft* (OHG, also called *Kasino*) was constituted by regimental bylaws, run by an elected officer board, staffed by enlisted *Ordonnanzen* (waiters/barmen), and financed by mess dues. Officers stood at attention upon entering the main hall. Pianos, recitals, and formal dinners were standard in pre-war Prussian, Bavarian, and Saxon garrisons; the *Kasino* continued in the field whenever quarters allowed.
- **Officers were systematically better fed.** The reference essay "Food and Nutrition" from 1914-1918-Online states explicitly: "**The officers took their meals separately and in more comfortable conditions than the soldiers. The quality of their meals and the interpersonal relationships of the officers reproduced the pre-war social hierarchy.**" Trench archaeology has recovered corked wine bottles and oyster shells from officer positions.
- **Field supplements and unit-level fiddling.** Ernst Jünger (then a lieutenant in the 73rd Hanoverian Fusiliers, later a *Stoßtrupp* company commander) records in *In Stahlgewittern* both the desiccated-vegetable "wire entanglements" of the line (diary entry, January 6, 1915: "filthy mess, probably stewed up out of frozen turnips") and the gourmet incident eight days later ("Wonderful bean soup, four rations, pangs of satiety"). The same diary records routine foraging: "the men were back from foraging trips of their own through the gardens."
- **The CO-discretion alcohol clause was the loophole.** Wine in summer for garrison troops; spirits in winter; beer routinely. Cavalry, *Jäger*, and Guards regiments in garrison towns drew on regimental wine cellars and stipulated mess wines (often Mosel, Rhein, or Franconian); officer messes on the Western Front systematically requisitioned Champagne and Burgundy, though this was less institutionalized in 1914–18 than during WWII, French and Belgian civilian accounts make clear that German officers in occupied territory had effective unilateral access to private cellars.
- **Sturmtruppen got double rations** between operations as a deliberate morale measure, Stoßtruppen "returned to the rear for training while receiving double rations, without performing duties." This was one of the most concrete combat-related food privileges in the army.
- **Local requisition authority** was, in principle, a corps- and division-level prerogative regulated by the Hague Conventions (which Germany signed in 1907 and routinely violated in Belgium in August 1914). In practice, any officer commanding troops in occupied territory could order "needed" supplies from the local mayor or *Bürgermeister* with a signed *Requisitionsschein*; reimbursement was theoretical.
- **Home parcels (*Liebesgaben* and family packages).** Officers received private parcels, chocolate, sausages, tinned goods, cigars, alcohol, from family and from regimental *Liebesgaben-Sammlungen*. Enlisted men received them too, but officers received them in greater quantity and via priority mail handled by their *Bursche*.
- **Wartime collapse.** Meat rations were cut progressively from June 1916 (one meatless day per week); by end-1916 fresh meat had fallen to 250 g and rear-area personnel got only 200 g. The 1916–17 "Turnip Winter" exposed the working class to acute hunger while officers' messes, buying on the black market, drawing on home estates, and requisitioning in occupied territories, remained comparatively well-supplied. A user of the Feldgrau forum, citing Roland Gaul's history of the 352. VGD, observed (admittedly for WW2, but echoing WWI practice): "The ration charts found in the US TM on the German army are what should be, a million miles away from what was the reality, troops simply ate what they could lay their hands on."

### 2. Transportation

- **Horses by rank (peacetime regulation).** Mounted officers were entitled to government-furnished riding horses up to specified counts by rank (e.g., a battalion commander to two chargers, a colonel three, a general up to six with carriages). Cavalry subalterns were issued their troop horse but maintained additional **privately owned chargers** at their own expense. Pre-war cavalry regiments, especially the 26 Uhlan regiments (three Guard, 16 Prussian line, two Württemberg, three Saxon, two Bavarian), actively recruited noble candidates with the lure of "depot locations near fashionable, large towns" and informal "additional income of 1,000 Mark per month" from one-year volunteers.
- **Wartime horse attrition.** In September 1916 cavalry regimental establishments were cut from 769 to 675 horses; entire regiments were dismounted and redesignated *Kavallerie-Schützen-Regimenter*. By 1918 only **22 of 110 active cavalry regiments remained mounted**, a fifth of the 1914 strength. The remaining mounted cavalry was concentrated on the Eastern Front, where the open spaces and weaker railway net permitted classic cavalry operations (e.g., the Courland and Vilna offensives of 1915 and the 1918 Ukraine intervention).
- **Railway travel.** Officers traveled first or second class on Reichsbahn military movement orders; enlisted men third or fourth. General Staff officers had near-unlimited travel authority; OHL maintained dedicated command trains. Hindenburg's August 1914 arrival at Hannover station to take command of 8th Army was on "a single locomotive with two coaches"; by the time he and Ludendorff held the Eastern command, they toured on "a staff train equipped with the most advanced communication apparatus."
- **Staff cars.** The *Kaiserliche Automobil-Korps* (the Kaiser's motor pool) and divisional/corps motor columns supplied senior officers. The 1916 Mercedes 16/40 "Knight Tourer" with its four-stroke Knight sleeve-valve engine was Wilhelm II's preferred command vehicle. Generals and chiefs of staff had personal drivers and dedicated touring cars; in 1914 most front-line officers still moved on horseback or by requisitioned civilian carriage. By 1917 the *Kraftfahrtruppen* had expanded dramatically and even battalion COs in some sectors had access to a borrowed staff car for inspections.
- **Bicycles, motorcycles.** Bicycle companies (*Radfahr-Kompanien*) were attached to *Jäger* battalions and cavalry divisions for reconnaissance; motorcycle dispatch riders (*Kradmelder*) were universal by 1916. Officers in technical branches (aviation, signals, pioneers) had broader latitude to procure motorcycles privately.
- **Private carriages and senior officers' use of civilian transport.** Generals on inspection routinely requisitioned civilian automobiles in Belgium and northern France; the Crown Prince's army group headquarters at **Stenay** (and earlier at Charleville-Mézières for OHL West) was notorious in French and Belgian post-war accounts for the lavish motor pool maintained for staff officers. (Sourcing on the specific Stenay vehicle establishment is thinner in English-language secondary literature than I could confirm in the time available.)

### 3. Gear and Equipment

- **Officer equipment was almost entirely private-purchase.** Regulation laid out the *Ausrüstung*, sword, sidearm, binoculars, map case, compass, whistle, pocket lamp, watch, but the officer paid for it himself, normally through a regimental military outfitter (e.g., the famous Berlin firms of Bonnesz, Tack, and others) or a *Hoflieferant* recommended by the regiment.
- **Binoculars, compasses, maps.** Carl Zeiss and Goerz binoculars (6×30 and 8×24) were near-universal among officers; the better Zeiss 8× models were status items. Maps were issued, but pocket compasses, range-finders, and personal stereoscopes were private.
- **Luxury field equipment.** Officer kits routinely included camp beds, folding furniture, washbasins, oil lamps, and lined sleeping bags from firms like Hagenuk or via Berlin emporiums. Junior officers traveled lighter; staff officers and senior commanders moved with substantial *Gepäck* carried by the unit train and unpacked by their *Bursche*.
- **Cameras.** Personal cameras (Goerz, Zeiss, Voigtländer plate folders) were permitted to officers; there was no equivalent of the strict British 1915 prohibition on cameras in the trenches, and German officer photo albums from the Western and Eastern Fronts survive in vast numbers. Reservist officers in particular documented everything.
- **The Bursche/Putzer system.** Every officer was assigned an enlisted *Ordonnanz*, colloquially *Bursche* or *Putzer*. Wikipedia summarizes: "In the German Army the batman was known as *Ordonnanz* (from the French *ordonnance*), or colloquially as *Putzer* ('cleaner') or as *Bursche* ('boy' or 'valet')." The *Bursche* carried the officer's spare kit, prepared his meals, and was effectively exempt from front-line combat duty when the officer was in the rear. Senior officers had multiple orderlies plus dedicated cooks.
- **Elite-unit equipment exemptions:** Stoßtruppen received Stahlhelme (1916) before the line; Austrian-pattern mountain boots and *Schnürschuhe* with leg-wraps replacing jackboots; reinforced leather elbow/knee patches; satchel grenades in mass quantities; specialized 37 mm and 7.5 cm infantry guns lightened by Rohr's team; flamethrowers (*Flammenwerfer*) operated by *Garde-Reserve-Pionier-Regiment*; and, late in the war, MP-18 submachine guns issued specifically to assault troops.
- **Foreign equipment.** Officers freely used Swiss Zeiss-derived optics, Austrian-pattern boots, and (after 1914) captured French and British equipment, including Webley revolvers, Lebel bayonets converted to trench knives, and British leather webbing all appear in photographs of front-line German officers.

### 4. Weapons

- **Officer sidearms.** The Pistole 08 (Luger) was the official sidearm; in practice "officers, many of whom paid for their own sidearms, obviously acquired the Luger in large numbers" (Recoil reference). Mauser C96 ("Broomhandle"), Mauser Pistole 1914, FN/Browning-pattern pocket pistols, and 7.65 mm civilian Lugers all appear in officer holsters. The Artillery Luger (LP08) with its 8" barrel and detachable stock, issued from 1914, was an artillery officers' carbine.
- **Swords.** The officer's *Degen* or sabre (model 1889 for infantry/cavalry officers) was a private purchase, often from Solingen makers (WKC, Eickhorn, Carl Eickhorn). Officers chose from a range of permitted blade styles and decorations within the regimental pattern; some regiments (Guards, hussars) had distinctive hilt and scabbard variations.
- **Hunting weapons at the front.** *Jäger* battalions, many of whose other ranks "had been gamekeepers, hunters, and even poachers" (War History Online on Rohr's recruitment pool), brought sporting rifles, scoped *Drillinge*, and shotguns to the front for sniping and trench-clearance. Officers' privately owned hunting rifles appear in trench photos of the Vosges and the Carpathians.
- **Trench-warfare weapons.** Trench daggers (*Grabendolche*), knuckle-dusters, and improvised clubs were widely tolerated despite no regulation authorization; Stoßtruppen formalized their use. Captured weapons such as Lewis guns, Webleys, Lebels, and Berthier carbines were privately retained and used.
- **Specialized assault unit weapons.** The Sturm-Bataillone formally fielded shortened Mauser carbines (Kar98AZ), MP18, sawn-off rifles, light Madsen guns (captured), 37 mm infantry guns, the Granatenwerfer 16, and the *Wechselapparat* (Wex) flamethrower, far broader than line infantry inventories.
- **Experimental weapons.** Officers at the Rohr training depot at Beuville and later Doberitz tested new weapons (light machine guns, flamethrowers, mortar variants); these privileges did not extend to line officers.

### 5. Uniform

- **Officers' uniforms were privately tailored.** Every officer purchased his own *Waffenrock* (dress tunic), *Litewka* (undress tunic), *Feldrock* (field tunic), greatcoat, riding breeches, boots, and headgear, usually from regimental tailors. By 1914 the M1910 field-grey tunic was theoretically standardized, but officers' versions used finer cloth (whipcord, doeskin) and were cut more closely. The *Litewka*, a long, double-breasted, turn-down-collar undress jacket, was a near-universal officer's working uniform, particularly for staff officers.
- **Commonly ignored regulations.** White piped Guards collars on field-grey were officially abolished by 1915 simplifications but persisted on officers' tailored tunics throughout. Coloured *Waffenfarbe* piping was discouraged in trenches but retained. Officers wore privately purchased high-quality riding boots well below the knee instead of regulation marching boots.
- **Headgear.** The *Pickelhaube* was theoretically covered with a grey cloth cover in the field from August 1914; many officers, especially in Bavaria and on the Eastern Front, retained polished officer-quality leather *Pickelhauben* without covers for staff functions and rear-area movement well into 1915. The Stahlhelm was introduced for Sturmbataillon Rohr in late 1915 and to the wider army in 1916, but its issue to officers was slower and many officers retained the lighter, more comfortable *Feldmütze* or *Schirmmütze* in trenches.
- **Cold-weather gear.** On the Eastern Front and in winter on the Western Front, officers wore privately purchased fur-lined coats, lambskin caps (especially in Carpathian and Galician operations), fur gloves, and felt overboots, none strictly regulation but universally tolerated.
- **Foreign-made items.** Captured British trench coats and Austrian alpine boots appear in officers' kit throughout the war. Some officers, particularly those who had served as military attachés abroad, kept English-style Sam Browne belts in addition to the regulation officer's brown leather belt.
- **Generals and field commanders.** Photographs show Hindenburg, Ludendorff, Falkenhayn, Mackensen, and Crown Prince Wilhelm in personally tailored grey field uniforms with regimental distinctives (Mackensen retained the Death's Head Hussars' fur cap throughout the war as his trademark), a privilege of personal eccentricity that no regulation specifically authorized but no one challenged.

### 6. Tactics and Operational Authority: *Auftragstaktik* in Practice

- **Doctrinal latitude.** *Auftragstaktik* (a term invented as a pejorative by its critics, preferring *Normaltaktik*) was central to Prussian-German military culture from the Moltke-era reforms. The Australian Army Research Centre's analysis quotes Gunther Rothenberg's definition: "a command method stressing decentralised initiative within an overall strategic framework." In doctrinal application this meant subordinates received the commander's *intent* (the mission) and the resources, but chose their own method.
- **Chief-of-staff dissent right.** Uniquely among 1914 European armies, "the chief of staff of a Prussian formation in the field had the right to disagree, in writing, with the plans or orders of the commander of the formation, and appeal to the commander of the next highest formation." This was both a real check on incompetence and a privilege of the General Staff caste, chiefs were imposed on hereditary line commanders specifically to provide brains where blood ruled.
- **Practical thresholds.** Company commanders (*Hauptleute*) had effective tactical independence within their *Gefechtsstreifen*; battalion commanders even more so, particularly in *Bewegungskrieg* (the early war and again in 1918). The Schlieffen Plan presupposed that subordinate army commanders would exercise such latitude that Helmuth von Moltke the Younger lost grip on First Army (von Kluck) in late August 1914, a textbook illustration of *Auftragstaktik* working both as intended and disastrously.
- **Sturmtruppen and tactical innovation.** Captain Willy Rohr's *Sturm-Bataillon Nr. 5* enjoyed extraordinary latitude to redesign small-unit tactics, weapons, footwear, and squad composition. Rohr "broke up [the unit] into very small squads of just a few men each, each led by a junior non-commissioned officer who had close communication with supporting weapons", formalizing the squad-based tactics that would inform Wehrmacht doctrine. Many of the changes were *first* implemented by Rohr's unit and only later imposed army-wide.
- **Cavalry independent operations.** Cavalry corps in 1914 (Höhere Kavallerie-Kommando) operated with broad screen-and-reconnaissance latitude, but the war's first weeks revealed that German cavalry generals, Marwitz, Richthofen, Hollen, had been trained for shock action that machine-gun and barbed-wire warfare made suicidal. Eastern Front cavalry retained genuine operational independence longer.
- **Aviation.** *Feldflieger-Abteilungen* (six aircraft each, attached to corps in 1914) had unusual latitude because no one above them understood the medium; this expanded as the *Luftstreitkräfte* matured. Fighter aces (Boelcke, Immelmann, Richthofen) operated under what amounted to celebrity privilege, selecting their own aircraft, mechanics, and tactics.

### 7. Leave and Privileges

- **Officers received substantially more and more flexible leave than enlisted men.** Pre-war regular officers had an annual entitlement of roughly four weeks; in wartime regular furlough for line officers ran 14 days every six months or so, but in practice frequency varied enormously by sector, unit, and the officer's personal connections. Compassionate leave was easier to obtain. Generals and staff officers came and went on liaison and inspection trips that doubled as home leave.
- **Reserve vs. regular officers.** Regular officers generally had priority for prestige postings and home leave; reserve officers (*Reserve-Offiziere*), who had qualified through the *Einjährig-Freiwilliger* path, sometimes resented their second-class treatment but enjoyed civilian-life privileges (continuing professional practice as lawyers, doctors, engineers, particularly during the long Eastern Front stalemates).
- **NCO leave** was less generous and more easily cancelled by operational need; enlisted men's home leave was rationed by the regimental adjutant and routinely cut during major operations. By 1917 leave was a significant morale grievance among the rank-and-file but remained relatively reliable for officers.
- **Married vs. unmarried.** Married officers received family-support allowances and were prioritized for home leave around childbirth and serious family illness; unmarried officers could be (and were) sent on emergency assignments instead.

### 8. Quarters and Accommodation

- **Billeting was rank-stratified.** German doctrine and the relevant Hague articles (with German reservations) gave occupying troops a right to billet on civilians. In practice each unit's quartermaster identified houses by quality and assigned them by rank: regimental commanders to the best villa or château in the village, company commanders to the local notable's house, the men to barns and outbuildings. Jünger's *Storm of Steel* describes precisely this: "The company had their customary barn quarters in the village of Trouville, one of the usual squalid hamlets of Lorraine," while platoon HQs and the company officers had requisitioned cottages.
- **Senior officers' headquarters.** Hindenburg–Ludendorff's first Eastern HQ was at Marienburg, then at the **citadel of Brest-Litovsk** (refurbished), then at Kovno. The Western OHL was at Charleville-Mézières (1914–18, with periods at Spa from 1918); the Crown Prince's 5th Army HQ at Stenay; the Bavarian Crown Prince Rupprecht's HQ at Tournai then Mons. These were not field tents but fully appropriated French and Belgian châteaux and public buildings, staffed by orderlies, cooks, signals personnel, and motor-pool drivers.
- **Hunting and recreation.** The German1914 historical site notes that Baltic German nobles "feted Hindenburg and he hunted their game preserves" while commanding Ober Ost (Hindenburg Wikipedia article). Game-shooting on requisitioned estates was a documented and tolerated perquisite of senior commanders East and West.
- **Quarters furnishings.** Officers brought or requisitioned furniture, rugs, paintings, and crockery. Period photographs of regimental HQs show pianos, libraries, and full dining services, none of which were issued.

### 9. Financial and Economic

- **Pay differentials were extreme.** A *Musketier* in 1914 earned a few Pfennigs per day; an *Unteroffizier* a low monthly stipend; a *Leutnant* a modest base salary that nominally could not support upper-middle-class life *without private income*. This was deliberate. The army assumed reserve and regular officers had inherited or family money; the *Einjährig-Freiwilliger* system existed precisely to filter for it. According to the Wikipedia summary of the system, in modern monetary value the self-equipping cost was "at bare minimum some 10,000 euro, which purposely reserved this path open to sons from mostly affluent social class families."
- **Mess fund and entertainment.** Each *Kasino* maintained a mess fund supplied by dues; officers' entertainment expenses (regimental balls, theater outings, dueling-society dinners) came from this fund or out-of-pocket. Lavish 1913 mess accounts from Berlin Guards regiments, preserved in regimental histories, show wine bills in the hundreds of Mark per month per officer.
- **Loans and debt.** Officer debt was a chronic problem; regimental honor courts could enforce settlement, and chronic indebtedness could end a career. Wartime moratoria protected serving officers from civil debt enforcement.
- **Prize money and captured goods.** Formally, captured material went to the state. In practice, officers retained captured pistols, binoculars, swords, watches, and (especially in the East) silverware and household goods; mass private appropriation of Belgian and northern French civilian property in August–September 1914 (the "rape of Belgium" period) was widely documented by Allied propaganda and confirmed by post-war Belgian and French claims commissions.
- **Private business and investments.** Reserve officers commonly retained civilian businesses, with proxies running them; regular officers were technically forbidden from active commercial activity but routinely managed family estates and investments from the front.

### 10. Social and Recreational

- **The *Kasino* and its bylaws.** The officer mess was the social center: dinners, recitals, balls, and the regimental *Stiftungsfest* (foundation day) were obligatory attendance. Junior officers' social acceptance depended on mess conduct.
- **Dueling.** The *Ehrenkodex* required officers to seek satisfaction for insults; the *Ehrennotwehr*, the "urgent necessity to defend honor", even required an officer to physically attack an insulting civilian. Wilhelm II's policy: punish duelists but cashier any officer who refused a challenge. The Mensur and dueling-fraternity culture (*Schmiss* scars as marks of caste) saturated the officer corps. The Pope had made dueling an excommunicable offense in 1867 and the Mensur was confirmed as a duel in 1890, both of which the German officer corps simply ignored. Jewish reserve officers used the dueling code in reverse, forcing Burschenschaft antisemites to choose between refusing satisfaction (and losing rank) or duelling them as equals.
- **Hunting.** Officers, especially aristocratic and *Jäger*-branch officers, pursued game whenever quartered in rural areas. *Jäger* tradition explicitly included gamekeepers and former poachers among its rank-and-file, and shooting parties for officers were a near-universal rear-area pastime.
- **Brothels and fraternization.** Officer brothels (*Offizierspuff*) were maintained as separate establishments from enlisted brothels (*Mannschaftspuff*), regulated by medical officers under venereal-disease control orders. Relationships with civilians in occupied territory were technically restricted but routine.
- **Alcohol.** The CO-discretionary alcohol clause translated, in practice, to nearly unlimited officer drinking in mess, regulated drinking for NCOs, and restricted but real spirits issue for the men.
- **Social hierarchy enforcement.** Junior officers were expected to defer to senior officers in non-duty settings; mess seating, addressing protocols, and even the order in which one mounted a horse were governed by regimental custom. Guard, cavalry, and Garde-Kürassier officers stood at the apex; line infantry below; *Train* (supply), heavy artillery, and pioneers traditionally the bottom of the prestige hierarchy. The Officer Corps essays at German1914 confirm: "All guard and cavalry regiments actively recruited nobles to keep the regiments as 'pure' as possible."

### 11. Branch-Specific Privileges

- **Guards (*Garde-Korps*, Berlin/Potsdam).** Highest prestige, near-exclusive noble officer corps in 1914, fanciest uniforms, best garrison towns, supplementary mess income from *Einjährig-Freiwillige*, closest access to the Kaiser. Guards officers' privately tailored uniforms were of demonstrably superior cloth.
- **Cavalry (Hussars, Uhlans, Dragoons, Cuirassiers, *Schwere Reiter*, *Chevaulegers*).** Most expensive to join (private chargers, fancy uniforms), most aristocratic, most rapidly obsolete in 1914 trench warfare. The Death's Head Hussars (Mackensen's regiment) were particularly prestigious.
- **Jäger battalions (24 in 1914).** Light infantry with distinctive grey-green uniforms; recruited from hunters, gamekeepers, and foresters; broad latitude on hunting weapons, marksmanship variations, and personalized field equipment. The pool from which Rohr drew his shock troops.
- **Foot artillery (*Fußartillerie*).** Heavy artillery, traditionally a non-noble branch, Junker prejudice held it beneath their dignity. By 1914 the most technically demanding branch and a key route to General Staff careers for talented commoners.
- **Pioneers (*Pioniere*).** Engineering specialists; before 1914 a low-prestige branch ("historically they were not soldiers at all," per German1914), but the *Pionier* battalions became the army's tactical laboratory, flamethrowers, gas, mines, fortification, and *Pionier* officers exercised great practical latitude on equipment.
- **Train and Supply (*Train-Bataillone*).** Lowest prestige, often staffed by commoner officers who had failed to enter combat branches; in wartime, the most logistically powerful and the most accused of profiteering and *Schiebung* (black-market dealing).
- **Aviation (*Fliegertruppe*, from 1916 *Luftstreitkräfte*).** New branch, broad latitude, glamorous; aces enjoyed celebrity privileges far beyond their formal rank.

### 12. Western vs. Eastern Front Differences

- **Western Front.** Static trench warfare from late 1914; officers quartered in French/Belgian villages and châteaux; ample requisition of wine, food, and household goods; cavalry largely dismounted; staff cars and motor transport common.
- **Eastern Front.** More mobile, more primitive infrastructure, harsher winters, greater officer latitude. Ober Ost under Hindenburg–Ludendorff functioned as a quasi-state with extensive forced labor, hunting privileges for senior officers on Baltic estates, and minimal civilian oversight. Cavalry retained genuine operational use longer. Lighter regulation of fraternization with local populations (Polish, Lithuanian, Baltic German).

## Recommendations

For a researcher, wargamer, novelist, reenactor, or instructor working with this material, I recommend the following staged approach:

1. **Anchor any narrative or simulation on the structural privilege of the officer corps, not on individual eccentricity.** The German officer's tailored *Litewka*, private Luger, requisitioned château, and *Bursche* were not personal foibles, they were institutional features of an aristocratic-bourgeois military caste financed by family money and unwritten regimental subsidies. If a single primary-source book is read, it should be one that captures this, Holger Herwig's *The First World War: Germany and Austria-Hungary 1914-1918* or Dennis Showalter's regimental and operational works.
2. **Distinguish ruthlessly between three different periods.** (a) Peacetime 1900–July 1914 garrison life, *Kasino* culture, dueling, *Junker* dominance, expensive parade uniforms. (b) Mobilization through end-1914, extraordinary supply abundance, near-Napoleonic looting in Belgium, full pre-war uniforms still in use. (c) Trench war 1915–1918, simplification of uniforms, rationing, Stoßtruppen experimentation, growing class friction. Conflating these three periods is the most common factual error in popular treatments.
3. **For the *biggest gaps between policy and reality*, prioritize four areas of further reading.** (i) The *Bursche*/orderly system (memoirs by junior officers, Jünger, Binding, von Salomon). (ii) Private weapons in field use (Luger forums and *Bewaffnung der deutschen Infanterie* literature). (iii) Officer requisition and quartering practice in Belgium and France (post-war Belgian and French claims commission records). (iv) Sturmbataillon Rohr equipment exemptions (Recio Cardona's *Sturmtruppen: WWI German Stormtroopers 1914-1918*, and Hauptmann Rohr's training notebook reproductions).
4. **If quantifying officer income, use multiple sources.** Pay tables alone underestimate by an order of magnitude; private estate income, family allowances, and regimental subsidies were the real economic basis of officer life. The €10,000 modern-equivalent figure for the *Einjährig-Freiwilliger* outfit cost is a floor, not a ceiling.
5. **Benchmarks that should change recommendations.** If new evidence shows the alcohol-discretion clause was rarely invoked in 1914 for elite units, the picture of differential drinking would soften. If captured French/Belgian archival data on requisition receipts becomes systematically available, the looting picture could be sharpened from impressionistic to statistical. Specific named documentation of Stenay or Charleville motor pools and Hindenburg's hunting bag at Ober Ost would similarly tighten the senior-officer-lifestyle case.

---

## Caveats

- **Sourcing depth varies sharply by topic.** The ration regulations are well-documented (Haythornthwaite's *World War One Sourcebook* tabulation is essentially canonical and is the basis for almost every online list). Officer mess culture, the *Bursche* system, Sturmtruppen equipment, *Auftragstaktik*, and the Hindenburg–Ludendorff lifestyle are well-attested. **Specific named historical examples of officer requisitioning, named hunting parties, named black-market transactions, and named officer leave patterns are thinner in English-language secondary sources** than the question implies; much of this material is in German regimental histories (the *Erinnerungsblätter deutscher Regimenter* series) and in unpublished or only partially digitized state archives. Where I have made statements without a named individual or document, treat them as the consensus picture of a well-attested institutional pattern rather than as a single citable fact.
- **The Crown Prince at Stenay and the OHL motor pool** are part of well-known war-memoir reputation but I was unable to confirm a specific quantitative claim about staff cars there during my searches.
- **The dueling regulations are paradoxical and the Kaiser's exact words exist in several formulations**; I have used the version that appears in the Pickelhauben.net summary, but variant phrasings appear in different secondary sources.
- **WWII French-wine requisition stories** (Klaebisch, de Vogüé, the 400,000-bottles-per-week champagne quota) are documented for 1940–44, not 1914–18. I have used them only as analogous illustration of the *kind* of requisition practice that existed in less systematic form during WWI, when German occupation of Champagne (Reims fell briefly in 1914, the front then settled west and north of it) prevented the same scale of organized looting.
- **The "double rations for Sturmtruppen between operations" detail** is reported in Historia Scripta's summary and is consistent with multiple specialist treatments, but the original Rohr-era documents are not reproduced in the sources I consulted.
- **My research budget was capped at fewer searches than ideal**, and the specialized subagent and draft-enrichment tools referenced in my task instructions were not actually available to me in this session. As a result, some sub-topics (notably named individual cases of regulation violation, primary-source quotations from German regimental histories, and quantitative breakdowns of officer leave) are summarized at the level of well-attested institutional pattern rather than down to verbatim citation. A reader requiring strictly verbatim documentary citation should treat this report as a high-quality secondary synthesis and pursue Holger Herwig, Dennis Showalter, Robert Citino's *The German Way of War*, Stephan Leistenschneider's *Auftragstaktik im preußisch-deutschen Heer 1871 bis 1914*, and Bruce Gudmundsson's *Stormtroop Tactics* as the primary scholarly anchors.


# Second Lieutenant (*Leutnant*) in the German Army 1914

## Position in the Hierarchy

**Bottom rung of the officer caste.** A *Leutnant* was the lowest commissioned rank, but still infinitely above the highest NCO (*Feldwebel*). The social gap between a second lieutenant and a sergeant was absolute, different species, not different ranks.

## What He Got (Official)

- Platoon command (roughly 50-60 men)
- Right to an enlisted *Bursche* (personal orderly)
- Officer mess membership
- Separate quarters from enlisted men
- Salute from all NCOs and enlisted
- Right to purchase and wear officer's uniform
- Authority to give orders to any enlisted man or NCO

## What It Cost Him

**Minimum €10,000 in today's money** to become a reserve *Leutnant* through the one-year volunteer path. That bought:

- Tailored uniforms (dress, field, greatcoat)
- Private sidearm (Luger P08 or equivalent)
- Sword
- Binoculars (Zeiss preferred)
- Map case, compass, whistle, watch
- Camp equipment
- Mess dues (ongoing)
- Social expenses (regimental balls, dinners)

**If joining Guards or cavalry:** Add another 1,000 Marks per month in "contributions" just to belong.

## His Daily Reality

**Peacetime garrison:**
- Lived in junior officers' quarters (shared room, maybe)
- Ate in officer mess (paid dues, stood when seniors entered)
- Drilled his platoon in the morning
- Attended obligatory social functions
- Was hazed by senior lieutenants
- Could not marry without permission and proof of independent income
- Fought duels if challenged or faced dismissal

**In the field (1914-1918):**
- Commanded his platoon in combat
- Billeted in a requisitioned cottage or room (not a château, those went to majors and up)
- His *Bursche* carried his gear, prepared meals, opened parcels from home
- Ate better than his men but not as well as captains
- Had tactical freedom within his sector under *Auftragstaktik*
- Could requisition civilian property with signed receipt
- Shared dugouts with other lieutenants in trenches
- Still attended mess dinners in rest areas

## The Brutal Truth

A *Leutnant* was **simultaneously privileged and poor.**

**Privileged** compared to his men, separate food, quarters, an orderly, authority, social standing.

**Poor** compared to senior officers, he had to *buy* his way into this world with family money, then maintain appearances on a salary that couldn't support it. Many junior officers were chronically in debt.

## Who Became One

**Regular army *Leutnant*:** Military academy graduate (cadet schools), usually minor nobility or upper-middle-class. Career path.

**Reserve *Leutnant*:** University-educated man who served one year as *Einjährig-Freiwilliger*, equipped himself entirely at own expense, passed officer exams. Called up in wartime. Often resented by regulars as "gentlemen playing soldier."

## Where He Sat Socially

**Above him (in order of increasing altitude):**
- *Oberleutnant* (First Lieutenant)
- *Hauptmann* (Captain), company commander, the first "real" command
- *Major*, battalion second-in-command
- *Oberstleutnant* (Lieutenant Colonel)
- *Oberst* (Colonel), regimental commander
- General officers

**Below him:**
- Every single NCO and enlisted man in the entire army, no exceptions

**Among fellow *Leutnants*:**
- Guards *Leutnant* > Cavalry *Leutnant* > Line Infantry *Leutnant* > Artillery *Leutnant* > Pioneer *Leutnant* > Train *Leutnant*
- Noble > commoner
- Regular > reserve
- Older commission date > newer

## His Actual Power

**Over his platoon:** Absolute. He could order field punishment, assign duties, approve leave, recommend decorations.

**In the regiment:** Almost none. Junior lieutenants stood at attention for everyone, said "yes sir," and absorbed the hazing until they had seniority over someone else.

**Tactically:** Under *Auftragstaktik*, significant. If his *Hauptmann* told him "take that trench," *how* he did it was his decision. This was real power, German junior officers had more initiative than British captains.

## The Contradiction

A second lieutenant was:
- Too poor to live as an officer (unless he had family money)
- Too proud to admit it
- Too junior to matter in regimental politics
- Too senior to ever speak to his men as equals again
- Powerful enough to get his platoon killed
- Powerless to change anything above platoon level

He was the **entry fee to the aristocratic caste,** paid in cash up front, with all the social burdens and only some of the privileges. Senior officers called him "the youngster." His men called him "Herr Leutnant" and would die if he told them to.

That was the position.
